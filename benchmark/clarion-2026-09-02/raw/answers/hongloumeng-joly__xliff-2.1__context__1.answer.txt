          <target>This is the opening chapter of the book. The author says: Having once passed through a dream-like illusion, he concealed the true events, and borrowing the tale of the "Spiritual Stone," wrote this book, The Story of the Stone. Hence the name "Zhen Shiyin" (True Events Concealed). But what events and what persons does the book record? He further says: "Now, weary and troubled in this dusty world, having accomplished nothing, I suddenly recalled all the women of former days. Examining them one by one in detail, I found that their conduct and insight all surpassed mine. How is it that I, a stalwart man, am truly inferior to those women in skirts and hairpins? I am filled with shame, and regret is useless—a day of utter helplessness! At such a time, I wished to compile a collection recounting the favors of Heaven and the virtues of my ancestors, the days of embroidered garments and fine silks, the times of rich food and sweet drink, the kindness of my father and elder brother in their instruction, the virtue of my teachers and friends in their admonitions—and my own faults, having achieved no skill to this day and spent half my life in ruin—to tell the world: my guilt indeed cannot be avoided, yet in the inner chambers there were truly remarkable women, and I must not, because of my own unworthiness and self-protective concealment of my shortcomings, let them perish together with me. Though today my dwelling is of thatched rafters and rush windows, with earthen stove and rope bed, the morning and evening wind and dew, the willows by the steps and flowers in the courtyard, do not hinder my heart and my pen. Though I am unlearned and my writing lacks literary grace, what harm is there in using rustic speech and village talk to spin out a tale, so that the women of the inner chambers may be made known, and the world may be delighted and their sorrows dispelled? Is this not fitting?" Hence the name "Jia Yucun" (False Words in Village Speech).</target>

          <source>空空道人遂向石頭說道：“石兄，你這一段故事，据你自己說有些趣味 ，故編寫在此，意欲問世傳奇．据我看來，第一件，無朝代年紀可考，第二 件，并無大賢大忠理朝廷治風俗的善政，其中只不過几個异樣女子，或情或 痴，或小才微善，亦無班姑，蔡女之德能．我縱抄去，恐世人不愛看呢。” 石頭笑答道：“我師何太痴耶！若云無朝代可考，今我師竟假借漢唐等年紀 添綴，又有何難？但我想，歷來野史，皆蹈一轍，莫如我這不借此套者，反 倒新奇別致，不過只取其事体情理罷了，又何必拘拘于朝代年紀哉！再者， 市井俗人喜看理治之書者甚少，愛适趣閒文者特多．歷來野史，或訕謗君相 ，或貶人妻女，奸淫凶惡，不可胜數．更有一种風月筆墨，其淫穢污臭，屠 毒筆墨，坏人子弟，又不可胜數．至若佳人才子等書，則又千部共出一套， 且其中終不能不涉于淫濫，以致滿紙潘安，子建，西子，文君，不過作者要 寫出自己的那兩首情詩艷賦來，故假擬出男女二人名姓，又必旁出一小人其 間撥亂，亦如劇中之小丑然．且鬟婢開口即者也之乎，非文即理．故逐一看 去，悉皆自相矛盾，大不近情理之話，竟不如我半世親睹親聞的這几個女子 ，雖不敢說強似前代書中所有之人，但事跡原委，亦可以消愁破悶，也有几 首歪詩熟話，可以噴飯供酒．至若离合悲歡，興衰際遇，則又追蹤躡跡，不 敢稍加穿鑿，徒為供人之目而反失其真傳者．今之人，貧者日為衣食所累， 富者又怀不足之心，縱然一時稍閒，又有貪淫戀色，好貨尋愁之事，那里去 有工夫看那理治之書？所以我這一段故事，也不愿世人稱奇道妙，也不定要 世人喜悅檢讀，只愿他們當那醉淫飽臥之時，或避世去愁之際，把此一玩， 豈不省了些壽命筋力？就比那謀虛逐妄，卻也省了口舌是非之害，腿腳奔忙 之．再者，亦令世人換新眼目，不比那些胡牽亂扯，忽离忽遇，滿紙才人 淑女，子建文君紅娘小玉等通共熟套之舊稿．我師意為何如？”</source>
          <target>The Taoist Kongkong (the Man of Emptiness) then said to the stone: "Brother Stone, this story of yours, as you yourself say, has some interest, and so you have written it here, wishing to send it forth to the world as a tale of wonder. But as I see it, first, it has no dynasty or era that can be verified; second, it contains no great sages or loyal ministers governing the court or reforming customs—only a few extraordinary women, some passionate, some infatuated, some with small talents and slight virtues, lacking the moral excellence of Ban Zhao or Cai Wenji. Even if I were to copy it out, I fear the world would not care to read it." The stone replied with a smile: "Master, why are you so foolish! If you say there is no dynasty to verify, what difficulty is there for you, Master, to simply borrow the reign names of the Han or Tang dynasties and add them? But I think that all the unofficial histories of the past have followed the same worn path. Better that mine, which does not borrow this convention, should be novel and distinctive—it merely takes the truth of events and human feeling, so why be bound by dynasty and era? Moreover, among the common people of the marketplace, few enjoy reading books of governance and morality, while many love light and entertaining writings. The unofficial histories of the past either slander rulers and ministers, or defame men's wives and daughters, with lewdness and villainy beyond count. There is also a kind of romantic writing, filthy and foul, poisoning the pen and corrupting the young, also beyond count. As for books of talented scholars and beautiful ladies, a thousand volumes all come from the same mold, and they cannot avoid descending into licentiousness, filling every page with Pan An, Zi Jian, Xi Shi, and Wenjun—merely because the author wishes to display his own two or three love poems and ornate rhapsodies, so he invents the names of a man and a woman, and must also introduce a villain to stir up trouble, like a clown in a play. Moreover, the maidservants speak in classical particles, always 'zhe,' 'ye,' 'zhi,' 'hu'—nothing but pedantry and moralizing. Read them one by one, and they are all self-contradictory, utterly lacking in human reason. They are not as good as these few women I have seen and heard with my own eyes in half a lifetime. Though I dare not say they surpass all the characters in earlier books, yet the full account of their deeds can dispel sorrow and break boredom, and there are a few crooked poems and familiar sayings that can make one laugh over food and drink. As for partings and reunions, grief and joy, rise and fall, I have followed the traces closely, not daring to add the slightest fabrication, lest I merely please the eye and lose the true transmission. The people of today—the poor are burdened daily by food and clothing, and the rich harbor discontent; even if they have a moment of leisure, they are occupied with lust and greed, coveting goods and seeking sorrow—where would they find the time to read books of governance? Therefore, in this story of mine, I do not wish the world to marvel at its wonders or praise its subtleties, nor do I insist that people delight in reading it. I only hope that when they are drunk, sated, and lying idle, or when they seek to escape the world and dispel their cares, they might play with this book—would that not save some of their life and strength? Compared to chasing after vain illusions, it would also spare them the harm of gossip and dispute, and the toil of running about. Moreover, it will open the world's eyes anew, unlike those old manuscripts full of random fabrications, sudden partings and meetings, and pages of talented men and virtuous ladies—Zi Jian, Wenjun, Hongniang, Xiaoyu—all the same stale conventions. What does my Master think of this?"</target>
