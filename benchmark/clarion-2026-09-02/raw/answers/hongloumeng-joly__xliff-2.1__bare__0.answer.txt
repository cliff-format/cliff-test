          <target>This is the opening chapter of the book. The author says: Having once passed through a dream-like experience, he concealed the true events and, borrowing the tale of the "Spiritual Stone," wrote this book, The Story of the Stone. Hence the name "Zhen Shiyin" (true events concealed). But what events and what people does the book record? He further says: "Now, busy and bustling in this dusty world, I have accomplished nothing. Suddenly I recall all the women of those days, and as I examine them one by one, I find their conduct and insight all superior to mine. How is it that I, a堂堂 man, am truly inferior to those women in skirts and hairpins? Truly I am filled with shame, and regret is useless—a day of utter helplessness! At such a time, I wish to compile a record of my past—the days when I relied on Heaven's grace and ancestral virtue, clad in brocade and fine silks, sated with rich food and sweet delicacies—and of my sins in turning my back on the kindness of my father and elder brothers, and failing the admonitions of my teachers and friends, until today I have not a single skill to show, and half my life is spent in ruin. I set this down to tell the world: my sins are indeed unavoidable, but among the women of the inner chambers there were truly remarkable persons, one by one. I must not, because of my own unworthiness and self-protective concealment of my faults, let them perish with me. Though today my dwelling is of thatched rafters and wicker windows, with earthen stove and rope bed, the morning and evening dew and wind, the willows by the steps and flowers in the courtyard, do not hinder my heart or my brush. Though I am unlearned and my writing lacks literary grace, what harm is there in using rustic language and village talk to spin out a tale, so that the women of the inner chambers may be made known to posterity, and the world's eyes be delighted and men's sorrows dispelled? Is this not fitting?" Hence the name "Jia Yucun" (fiction in rustic language).</target>

Even if I were to copy it out, I fear the world would not care to read it." The stone replied with a smile: "Master, why are you so foolish! If you say there is no dynasty to verify, what difficulty is there for you, Master, to borrow the names of the Han or Tang dynasties and add them on? But I think that all the unofficial histories of the past have followed the same mold. Better that mine, which does not borrow this convention, should be novel and distinctive. I merely take the truth of events and human feeling—why cling so rigidly to dynasty and era? Moreover, among the common folk of the marketplace, few enjoy books on governance and morality, while many delight in light and entertaining reading. The unofficial histories of the past either slander rulers and ministers, or defame men's wives and daughters, full of lewdness and villainy beyond count. There is also a kind of amorous writing, filthy and foul, poisoning the page and corrupting the young, beyond count. As for books of gifted scholars and beautiful ladies, a thousand volumes all come from the same mold, and they cannot avoid descending into licentiousness, filling the page with Pan An, Cao Zijian, Xi Shi, and Zhuo Wenjun—merely because the author wishes to show off his own two love poems and flowery rhapsodies, so he invents the names of a man and a woman, and must also bring in a minor villain to stir up trouble, like a clown in a play. And the maidservants speak in classical phrases, nothing but pedantry and moralizing. Read them one by one, and they are all self-contradictory, utterly lacking in plausibility. They are not worth these few women I have seen and heard with my own eyes in half a lifetime. Though I dare not say they surpass all the characters in earlier books, yet the full account of their deeds can dispel sorrow and relieve boredom, and there are a few crooked poems and familiar sayings that can make one laugh over food and wine. As for partings and reunions, joys and sorrows, rises and falls, I have followed the traces closely, not daring to add the slightest embellishment, lest I please the eye and lose the true transmission. The people of today—the poor are burdened daily by food and clothing, the rich harbor discontent, and even when they have a moment of leisure, they are taken up with lust and desire, greed for goods and the pursuit of worry—where would they find the time to read books on governance?
