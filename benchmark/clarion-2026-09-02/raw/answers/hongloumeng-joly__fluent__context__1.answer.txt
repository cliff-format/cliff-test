chapter-1-001 = This is the opening chapter of the book. The author says: Having once passed through a dream-like illusion, he therefore concealed the true events, and borrowing the tale of the "Spiritual Stone," wrote this book, The Story of the Stone. Hence the name "Zhen Shiyin" and so forth. But what events and what persons are recorded in this book? He further says: "Now, weary and troubled in this dusty world, having accomplished nothing, I suddenly thought of all the women of former days. Examining them one by one in detail, I felt that their conduct and insight were all superior to mine. How is it that I, a dignified man, am truly inferior to those women in skirts and hairpins? Truly I am filled with shame, and regret is of no avail—a day of utter helplessness! At such a time, I wished to compile an account of the crimes of my past—the days when I relied on Heaven's grace and ancestral virtue, clad in embroidered silks and fine garments, sated with sweet and rich foods, turning my back on the kindness of my father and elder brother's instruction, failing the virtue of my teachers' and friends' admonitions—until today, when I have not a single skill to show and half my life has been spent in ruin. I set this down to tell the world: my guilt indeed cannot be excused, yet in the inner chambers there were those who truly existed, one by one, and I must not, because of my own unworthiness and self-protecting of my shortcomings, let them all be obliterated. Though today my thatched rafters and rush windows, my earthen stove and rope bed, with morning and evening wind and dew, willows by the steps and flowers in the courtyard, do not hinder my heart and my brush. Though I am unlearned and my writing lacks literary grace, what harm is there in using rustic speech and village talk to spin out a tale, so that the women of the inner chambers may be made known to posterity, and the world's eyes be delighted and men's sorrows dispelled? Is this not fitting?" Hence the name "Jia Yucun" and so forth.

chapter-1-004 = The Taoist Kongkong then said to the stone: "Brother Stone, this story of yours, according to what you yourself say, has some interest, and so you have written it here, wishing to bring it before the world as a tale of wonder. As I see it, first, there is no dynasty or date to be verified; second, there are no great sages or loyal ministers governing the court or improving customs—only a few extraordinary women, some passionate, some infatuated, some with small talents and slight virtues, lacking the virtue and ability of Ban Gu or Cai Wenji. Even if I were to copy it out, I fear the world would not care to read it." The stone replied with a smile: "Master, why are you so foolish! If you say there is no dynasty to be verified, what difficulty is there for you, Master, to borrow the reigns of Han or Tang and add them as embellishment? But I think that all the unofficial histories of the past have followed the same worn path. Better that mine, which does not borrow this convention, should be novel and distinctive instead. It merely takes the truth of events and human feeling—why must it be bound by dynasty and date! Moreover, among the common people of the marketplace, few delight in books of governance and morality, while many love idle writings of amusement. The unofficial histories of the past either slander rulers and ministers, or defame men's wives and daughters—their lewdness, wickedness, and violence are beyond counting. There is also a kind of romantic writing whose filth and stench, whose murderous and poisonous pen, corrupts the young—also beyond counting. As for books of handsome scholars and beautiful ladies, a thousand volumes all come from the same mold, and moreover they cannot avoid descending into licentiousness, so that the pages are filled with Pan An, Zijian, Xizi, and Wenjun—merely because the author wishes to display his own two or three amorous poems and flowery rhapsodies, and so invents the names of a man and a woman, and must also bring in a villain to stir up trouble between them, like the clown in a play. And the maidservants, the moment they open their mouths, speak in classical particles—nothing but literary and moralizing talk. Thus, read one after another, they are all self-contradictory and utterly unreasonable. They are not as good as these few women I have seen and heard with my own eyes in half a lifetime. Though I dare not say they are superior to all the people in the books of former ages, yet the events and their causes can dispel sorrow and break boredom, and there are also a few crooked verses and familiar sayings that can make one laugh over one's rice and wine. As for partings and reunions, grief and joy, the rise and fall of fortunes, I have followed the traces closely, not daring to add the slightest fabrication, merely to please men's eyes and thereby lose the true transmission. The people of today—the poor are daily burdened by food and clothing, and the rich harbor discontented hearts. Even if they have a moment of leisure, there are matters of lust and lechery, of coveting goods and seeking sorrow—where would they find the time to read books of governance? Therefore, in this story of mine, I do not wish the world to marvel at its wonders or praise its subtleties, nor do I insist that the world delight in reading it. I only wish that when they are drunk, wanton, sated, and lying down, or when they are fleeing the world and seeking to forget their sorrows, they might take this up for amusement—would that not save some of their lifespan and strength? Compared with pursuing vanity and chasing delusion, it would also spare them the harm of gossip and dispute, and the toil of running about. Moreover, it will open the world's eyes to something new, unlike those old manuscripts full of forced connections and sudden partings and meetings, filled with gifted scholars and virtuous ladies, Zijian, Wenjun, Hongniang, Xiaoyu, and all the familiar stock characters. What does my Master think of this?"

chapter-1-005 = The Taoist Kongkong, hearing these words, pondered for a long while, then examined The Story of the Stone once more from beginning to end. He saw that although it contained some words pointing at treachery, censuring the wicked, and condemning evil, it was not in the spirit of attacking the times or cursing the world. Rather, wherever it touched upon the kindness of rulers and the goodness of ministers, the compassion of fathers and the filial piety of sons—all matters of the cardinal relationships—it was full of praise for merit and virtue, with endless affection, truly incomparable to other books. Because it did not in the least interfere with the affairs of the age, he copied it out from beginning to end and brought it before the world as a tale of wonder. chapter-1-006 = In those days, when the earth tilted toward the southeast, there was in that southeastern corner a place called Gusu, with a city gate called Changmen, which was one of the foremost places of wealth, rank, and elegance in the dusty world. Outside this Changmen gate there was a Ten Li Street, and in the street there was a Renqing Lane, and in the lane there was an old temple. Because the place was narrow and cramped, everyone called it Gourd Temple. Beside the temple lived a retired local official, surnamed Zhen, named Fei, with the courtesy name Shiyin. His lawful wife, née Feng, was of a virtuous and gentle disposition, deeply versed in propriety and righteousness. Though the family was not greatly wealthy, yet in that locality they were regarded as a distinguished clan. Because this Zhen Shiyin was by nature tranquil and indifferent, not concerned with fame and advancement, his daily pleasure consisted only of viewing flowers, trimming bamboo, drinking wine, and chanting poetry—truly a man of immortal caliber. There was but one deficiency: now past fifty years of age, he had no son at his knee, only a daughter, whose milk name was Yinglian, just three years old.

chapter-1-014 = A mean hut and an empty hall—once the tablets of office filled the beds. Withered grass and decaying willows—once the place of song and dance. Spider webs now festoon the carved beams; green gauze is now pasted over the rush windows. What talk of rouge being thick, of powder being fragrant—how is it that both temples have turned to frost? Yesterday, at the head of the yellow-earth mound, white bones were sent off; tonight, beneath the red-lamp canopy, mandarin ducks lie together. Gold fills the chests, silver fills the chests—in the blink of an eye, a beggar, and all men revile him. Just as one sighs that another's life is short, who knows that one's own return will be death! To train one's children with discipline—there is no guarantee they will not become brigands in the future. To choose a fine match from the rich—who could have expected the daughter to fall into the alley of painted ladies! Because one grudges the smallness of the gauze cap of office, one ends up bearing the cangue and chains. Yesterday one pitied the cold of a tattered padded jacket; today one finds the purple court robe too long. In the confusion, no sooner have you finished singing than I make my entrance—mistaking a foreign land for one's native home. Utterly absurd—in the end, it is all making wedding clothes for others! When the mad lame Taoist heard this, he clapped his hands and laughed, saying: "Well annotated, well annotated!" Shiyin then said the single word "Let's go!" and snatched the cloth sack from the Taoist's shoulder, slinging it on his own back. He did not return home, but drifted away together with the mad Taoist. At once the whole neighborhood was stirred up, and everyone spread the story as a piece of news. When Lady Feng heard of this, she wept until she nearly died, and could only consult with her father, sending people to search everywhere—but what news could be found? Having no alternative, she had to depend on her parents for her daily living. Fortunately, she still had two old maidservants to wait on her. The three of them, mistress and maids, spent their days and nights doing needlework to sell, helping with her father's expenses. Though Feng Su complained daily, there was nothing he could do about it.
