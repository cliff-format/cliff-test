    <string name="chapter-1-001">This is the opening chapter of the book. The author says: Having once passed through a dreamlike illusion, I have concealed the true events, and borrowing the tale of the "Spiritual Stone," have written this book, The Story of the Stone. Hence the name "Zhen Shiyin" and so forth. But what events and what persons does this book record? Again he says: "Now, weary and troubled by the dust of the world, having accomplished nothing, I suddenly thought of all the women of former days; examining them one by one in detail, I felt that their conduct and insight were all superior to mine. How is it that I, a dignified man, am truly inferior to those women in skirts and hairpins? Truly shameful beyond measure, and regret is of no avail—a day of utter helplessness! At this time, I wished to take the favors of Heaven's grace and ancestral virtue I once relied upon, the days of embroidered clothes and fine trousers, the times of sweet food and rich fare, and, turning my back on the kindness of my father and elder brother's instruction, and failing the virtue of my teachers' and friends' admonitions, down to this day when I have not mastered a single skill and have wasted half my life—to compile these faults into a collection, to tell the world: my sins are indeed unavoidable, but in the inner chambers there were truly remarkable persons; I must not, because of my own unworthiness, shield my own shortcomings and let them be obliterated along with me. Though today my dwelling is of thatched rafters and reed windows, with earthen stove and rope bed, the morning breeze and evening dew, the willows by the steps and flowers in the courtyard, do not hinder my heart or my pen. Though I am unlearned and my writing lacks literary grace, what harm is there in using rustic speech and village talk to spin out a tale, so that the women of the inner chambers may be made known, and the world's eyes be delighted and men's sorrows dispelled? Is this not fitting?" Hence the name "Jia Yucun" and so forth.</string>

    <string name="chapter-1-004">The Taoist Kongkong then said to the stone: "Brother Stone, this story of yours, as you yourself say, has some interest, and so you have written it here, wishing to bring it to the world as a tale. But as I see it, first, there is no dynasty or date to be verified; second, there are no great sages or loyal ministers governing the court or improving customs—only a few extraordinary women, some passionate, some infatuated, some with small talents and slight virtues, none possessing the virtue and ability of Ban Gu or Cai Wenji. Even if I were to copy it out, I fear the world would not care to read it." The stone replied with a smile: "Master, why are you so foolish! If you say there is no dynasty to verify, what difficulty is there for you, Master, to borrow the reigns of Han or Tang and add them? But I think that all the unofficial histories of the past have followed the same rut; none is as novel and distinctive as mine, which does not borrow this convention, but merely takes the truth of events and human feeling—why cling so rigidly to dynasty and date? Moreover, few among the common people delight in books of governance; far more love amusing and idle writings. The unofficial histories of the past either slander rulers and ministers, or defame men's wives and daughters, with lewdness and villainy beyond count. There is also a kind of romantic writing, filthy and foul, poisoning the pen and corrupting the young, beyond count. As for books of gifted scholars and beauties, a thousand volumes all come from the same mold, and in them one cannot avoid lewd excess, so that the pages are filled with Pan An, Zijian, Xishi, and Wenjun—merely because the author wishes to display his own two or three love poems and ornate rhapsodies, and so invents the names of a man and a woman, and must also bring in a villain to stir up trouble, like the clown in a play. And the maidservants, as soon as they open their mouths, speak in classical phrases—nothing but letters and reason. Thus, reading them one by one, they are all self-contradictory and utterly unreasonable. They are not as good as these few women I have seen and heard with my own eyes in half a lifetime. Though I dare not say they surpass all the persons in earlier books, yet the events and their causes can dispel sorrow and break boredom, and there are a few crooked poems and familiar sayings that can make one laugh over food and drink. As for partings and reunions, joys and sorrows, rises and falls, I have followed the traces closely, not daring to add the slightest fabrication, merely to please the eye and lose the true transmission. The people of today—the poor are burdened daily by food and clothing, the rich harbor discontent; even if they have a moment of leisure, there are matters of lust and love, greed and worry—where would they find the time to read books of governance? Therefore, in this story of mine, I do not wish the world to marvel at its wonders, nor do I insist that the world delight in reading it; I only wish that when they are drunk, lustful, full, and lying down, or when they seek to escape the world and dispel their sorrows, they may play with this book—would that not save some of their life and strength? Compared to chasing after vain illusions, it would also save them the harm of gossip and dispute, and the toil of running about. Moreover, it will open the world's eyes anew, unlike those old manuscripts full of gifted men and fair ladies, Zijian, Wenjun, Hongniang, Xiaoyu, and all the familiar stock characters, with their wild fabrications and sudden partings and meetings. What does my Master think of this?"</string>

    <string name="chapter-1-005">The Taoist Kongkong, hearing this, pondered for a while, then examined The Story of the Stone once more.     <string name="chapter-1-006">In those days, when the earth tilted toward the southeast, in that southeastern corner there was a place called Gusu, with a city gate named Changmen, which was one of the foremost places of wealth and elegance in the mortal world. Outside this Changmen there was a Ten Li Street, and in the street an Alley of Benevolent Purity, and in the alley an old temple, which, being narrow and cramped, everyone called Gourd Temple. Beside the temple lived a local official, surnamed Zhen, named Fei, with the courtesy name Shiyin. His lawful wife, née Feng, was virtuous and gentle, deeply versed in propriety and righteousness. Though the family was not exceedingly wealthy, they were regarded locally as a distinguished house. Because Zhen Shiyin was by nature tranquil and indifferent, not concerned with fame or office, he spent his days delighting in viewing flowers, trimming bamboos, drinking wine, and composing poems—truly a man of immortal quality. There was but one deficiency: though he was now past fifty, he had no son, only a daughter, whose milk name was Yinglian, just three years old.</string>

    <string name="chapter-1-014">A mean hut and an empty hall, where once the tablets of office filled the bed; withered grass and dying willows, where once was a place of song and dance. Spider webs now knot the carved beams; green gauze is pasted again over the reed window. What talk of rouge being thick, of powder being fragrant—how is it that both temples have turned to frost? Yesterday, at the head of the yellow-earth mound, white bones were sent off; tonight, beneath the red-lamp canopy, mandarin ducks lie together. Gold fills the chest, silver fills the chest—but in the blink of an eye, a beggar, and all men revile him. Just as one sighs that another's life is short, who knows that one's own return brings death! To train one's sons with discipline—who can guarantee they will not become brigands? To choose a wealthy match—who would expect the daughter to fall into the pleasure quarters! Because one grudges the smallness of the gauze cap, one ends up bearing the cangue and chains; yesterday one pitied the cold of a tattered coat, today one grudges the length of the purple robe. In the turmoil, you have just finished singing and I mount the stage, mistaking a foreign land for one's native home. How absurd—in the end, all is but making wedding clothes for others! When the mad lame Taoist heard this, he clapped his hands and laughed, saying: "Well explained, well explained!" Shiyin then said, "Let us go!" and snatched the cloth bag from the Taoist's shoulder, slung it on his own back, and without returning home, drifted away with the mad Taoist. At once the neighborhood was stirred, and everyone spread the tale as a piece of news. When Fengshi heard of this, she wept as if she would die, and could only consult with her father, sending people to search everywhere—but what news could be found? Helpless, she had no choice but to depend on her parents for her daily needs. Fortunately, she still had two old maidservants to attend her; the three of them, mistress and maids, worked day and night at needlework to sell, helping her father with expenses. Though Feng Su complained daily, he too could do nothing about it.</string>
