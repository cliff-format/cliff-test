msgstr "This is the first chapter of the book. The author himself says: Having once passed through a dream-like illusion, he concealed the true events, and borrowing the tale of the \"Spiritual Stone,\" composed this book, the Story of the Stone. Hence the name \"Zhen Shiyin\" (True Events Concealed). But what events and what persons does the book record? He further says: \"Now, weary and troubled in this dusty world, having accomplished nothing, I suddenly bethought myself of all the women of former days, and examining them one by one in detail, I found that their conduct and insight all surpassed my own. How is it that I, a stalwart man, am truly inferior to those in skirts and hairpins? It is a day of shame beyond measure, of regret that avails nothing, of utter helplessness! At such a time, I wished to compile a record of the sins of my past—the days when, relying on Heaven's grace and ancestral virtue, I wore embroidered garments and rich furs, feasted on sweet and savory fare, turned my back on the kindness of my father and elder brother's instruction, and failed the virtue of my teachers' and friends' admonitions—until today, having mastered no single skill and spent half my life in ruin. This I set down to tell the world: my guilt indeed cannot be excused, yet in the inner chambers there were truly remarkable persons, and I must not, because of my own unworthiness and self-protecting faults, let them all perish with me. Though today my dwelling is of thatched rafters and rush windows, with earthen stove and rope bed, the morning and evening wind and dew, the willows by the steps and flowers in the court, do not hinder my heart or my brush. Though I am unlearned and my pen lacks literary grace, what harm is there in using rustic speech and village talk to spin out a tale, that the women of the inner chambers may be made known, the world's eyes be delighted, and men's sorrows dispelled? Is this not fitting?\" Hence the name \"Jia Yucun\" (False Words in Village Speech)."

It merely takes the truth of events and human feeling—why be so bound to dynasty and era! Moreover, few among the common folk delight in books of governance, while many love idle and amusing writings. The unofficial histories of the past either slander rulers and ministers, or defame men's wives and daughters—lewdness and villainy beyond count. There is also a kind of amorous writing, filthy and foul, poisonous to the pen and corrupting to the young, beyond count. msgstr "A mean hut, an empty hall—in former days the tablets of office filled the bed. Withered grass, dried willows—this was once a place of song and dance. Spider webs now knot the carved beams; green gauze is pasted again on the rush windows. What talk of rouge being thick, of powder being fragrant? How is it that both temples have turned to frost? Yesterday, at the head of the yellow-earth mound, they sent off white bones; tonight, beneath the red-lamp canopy, they lie as mandarin ducks. Gold fills the chest, silver fills the chest—in the blink of an eye, a beggar, and all men revile him. Just as one sighs that another's life is short, who knows that one's own return will be death! To train one's sons with discipline—who can guarantee they will not become brigands in the future? To choose a fine match among the rich—who would expect the daughter to drift into the lanes of the pleasure quarters! Because one grudges the gauze cap as too small, one ends up bearing the cangue and chains. Yesterday one pitied the tattered coat for its cold; today one dislikes the purple robe for its length. In the confusion, just as you finish singing, I take the stage—mistaking a strange land for one's native home. How utterly absurd! In the end, it is all but making wedding clothes for others! When the mad lame Taoist heard this, he clapped his hands and laughed, saying: \"Well annotated, well annotated!\" Shiyin then said but one word: \"Let us go!\" He snatched the cloth sack from the Taoist's shoulder and carried it on his own back, and without returning home, went off drifting away with the mad Taoist. At once the whole neighborhood was stirred, and everyone spread the tale as a piece of news. When Lady Feng heard of this, she wept as if she would die, and could only consult with her father, sending people to search everywhere—but what news could be had? With no help for it, she had to depend on her parents for her daily keep. Fortunately, she still had two old maidservants to attend her, and the three of them, mistress and maids, worked day and night at needlework to sell, helping with her father's expenses. Though Feng Su grumbled daily, there was nothing he could do."
