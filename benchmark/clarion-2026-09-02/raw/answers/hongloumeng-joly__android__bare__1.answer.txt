    <string name="chapter-1-001">This is the first chapter of the book. The author says: Having once passed through a dreamlike experience, he concealed the true events and, borrowing the tale of the "Spiritual Understanding," wrote this book, The Story of the Stone. Hence the name "Zhen Shiyin" and so forth. But what events and what persons does the book record? He further says: "Now, weary and busy in the world's dust, having accomplished nothing, I suddenly recall all the women of former days. Examining them one by one, I find their conduct and insight all superior to mine. How is it that I, a dignified man, am truly inferior to those in skirts and hairpins? Indeed, shame is more than enough, and regret is useless—a day of utter helplessness! At such a time, I wish to compile a collection recounting the sins of relying on Heaven's grace and ancestral virtue, of the days of embroidered clothes and fine silks, of feasting on delicacies, of turning my back on the kindness of father and elder brother's instruction, of failing the virtue of teachers and friends' admonitions, until today I have achieved no skill and spent half my life in decline—to tell the world: my sins are indeed unavoidable, but in the inner chambers there were truly remarkable persons, and I must not, because of my own unworthiness and self-protection, let them be obliterated. Though today my thatched roof and wicker windows, earthen stove and rope bed, with morning and evening wind and dew, stepside willows and courtyard flowers, do not hinder my heart and pen. Though I am unlearned and my writing lacks refinement, what harm is there in using rustic language and village talk to spin out a tale, so that the inner chambers may be made known, the world's eyes delighted, and people's sorrows dispelled? Is that not fitting?" Hence the name "Jia Yucun" and so forth.</string>

    <string name="chapter-1-004">The Taoist Kongkong then said to the stone: "Brother Stone, this story of yours, as you yourself say, has some interest, so you have written it here, wishing to bring it to the world as a tale. As I see it, first, there is no dynasty or era to verify; second, there are no great sages or loyal ministers governing the court or reforming customs—only a few extraordinary women, some passionate, some infatuated, some with minor talents and small virtues, lacking the moral excellence of Ban Zhao or Cai Wenji. Even if I copied it out, I fear the world would not care to read it." The stone replied with a smile: "Master, why are you so foolish! If you say there is no dynasty to verify, what difficulty is there for you to borrow the eras of Han or Tang and add them? But I think that all unofficial histories of the past have followed the same pattern; better that mine, which does not borrow this convention, should be novel and distinctive. It merely takes the essence of events and human feelings—why cling rigidly to dynasty and era? Moreover, few among the common folk enjoy reading books on governance; far more delight in amusing and entertaining writings. Past unofficial histories either slander rulers and ministers or defame men's wives and daughters, with lewdness and villainy beyond count. There is also a kind of romantic writing, filthy and corrupting, poisoning the pen and ruining the young, also beyond count. As for tales of gifted scholars and beautiful ladies, a thousand volumes all follow the same formula, and inevitably they descend into licentiousness, filling pages with Pan An, Zijian, Xi Shi, and Wenjun—merely because the author wishes to display his own amorous verses, so he invents the names of a man and a woman, and must also introduce a villain to stir up trouble, like a clown in a play. And the maidservants speak in classical phrases, always literary and moralistic. Reading them one by one, they are all self-contradictory and utterly implausible. They are not as good as these few women I have personally seen and heard in my half lifetime. Though I dare not say they surpass all the characters in earlier books, their deeds and their origins can dispel sorrow and relieve boredom, and there are a few clumsy verses and familiar sayings that can accompany a meal or a cup of wine. As for partings and reunions, joys and sorrows, rises and falls, I have followed the traces closely, not daring to add the slightest embellishment, lest I merely please the eye and lose the true transmission. People today—the poor are burdened daily by food and clothing, the rich harbor discontent; even when they have a moment of leisure, they are occupied with lust, greed, and the pursuit of worries—where would they find time to read books on governance? Therefore, I do not expect the world to marvel at this story or praise its excellence, nor do I insist that people delight in reading it. I only hope that when they are drunk, sated, and lying idle, or when they seek to escape the world and dispel their cares, they might play with this a while—would that not save some of their life and strength? Compared with chasing illusions and vain pursuits, it would also spare them the harm of gossip and contention, and the toil of running about. Moreover, it will open the world's eyes anew, unlike those old manuscripts full of random fabrications, sudden partings and meetings, and pages of gifted men and fair ladies—Zijian, Wenjun, Hongniang, Xiaoyu—all the familiar stale conventions.
