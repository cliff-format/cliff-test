The author says: Having once passed through a dream-like experience, he concealed the true events and, borrowing the tale of the \"Spiritual Stone,\" wrote this book, The Story of the Stone. Hence the name \"Zhen Shiyin\" (True Events Concealed). But what events and persons does the book record? He further says: \"Now, bustling about in this dusty world, I have accomplished nothing. Suddenly I recall all the women of former days, and examining them one by one, I find their conduct and insight all surpass my own. How is it that I, a stalwart man, am truly inferior to those fair maids? Indeed, shame is more than enough, and regret is of no avail—a day of utter helplessness! At this time, I wish to take the favors of Heaven's grace and ancestral virtue, the days of silken robes and rich fare, the kindness of my father and elder brother's instruction, the virtue of my teachers and friends' admonitions—all of which I have failed—and, having to this day no single skill and a life half spent in ruin, compile a collection to tell the world: my guilt cannot be excused, yet among the inner chambers there have indeed been remarkable persons. I must not, because of my own unworthiness, conceal my faults and let them perish with me. Though today my thatched roof and wicker windows, my earthen stove and rope bed, the morning breeze and evening dew, the willows by the steps and flowers in the court, do not hinder my heart and pen. Though I am unlearned and my writing lacks refinement, what harm is there in using rustic speech and village talk to spin out a tale, that the inner chambers may be made known, the world's eyes delighted, and men's sorrows dispelled? Is this not fitting?\" Hence the name \"Jia Yucun\" (False Words in Village Speech)."

But I think that all unofficial histories of the past have followed the same rut; better that mine, which does not borrow this convention, should be novel and distinctive. It merely takes the truth of events and human feeling—why cling so rigidly to dynasty and era? The unofficial histories of the past either slander rulers and ministers or defame men's wives and daughters, with lewdness and villainy beyond count. They are not as good as these few women I have seen and heard with my own eyes in half a lifetime. Though I dare not say they surpass all the characters in earlier books, yet the full account of their deeds can dispel sorrow and break melancholy, and there are a few crooked verses and familiar sayings that can provoke laughter over wine. As for partings and reunions, joys and sorrows, rises and falls, I have followed the traces closely, not daring to add the slightest fabrication, lest I merely please the eye and lose the true transmission. The people of today—the poor are burdened daily by food and clothing, the rich harbor discontent; even when briefly at leisure, they are occupied with lust and greed, coveting goods and seeking sorrow. Where would they find time to read books of governance? Therefore, in this tale of mine, I do not wish the world to marvel at its wonder, nor do I insist that they delight in reading it. I only hope that when they are drunk, sated, and lying idle, or when they seek to escape the world and dispel their cares, they may amuse themselves with it—would that not save some of their life's strength? Compared to scheming after vanity and chasing illusions, it would also spare them the harm of gossip and strife, and the toil of running about.
