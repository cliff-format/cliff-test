    <string name="chapter-1-001">This is the opening chapter of the book. The author himself declares: because he had once passed through a dream and an illusion, he therefore conceals the true facts, and, borrowing the tale of the "Spiritual Understanding," writes this book called The Story of the Stone. Hence it is called "Zhen Shiyin" (True Facts Concealed), and so on. But what matters, and what persons, are recorded in the book? He says again of himself: "Now, toiling and moiling in the dust of the world and having accomplished nothing, I suddenly bethought me of all the maidens of former days, and, examining them carefully one by one, I felt that their conduct and understanding were all above my own. Why is it that I, with my stalwart beard and brows, am truly not the equal of those girls in skirts and hairpins? Truly, shame is what I have in abundance, and repentance is of no avail; there is nothing to be done on such a day as this! And so I wished to set down in a volume the times when I relied upon the grace of Heaven and the virtue of my ancestors, the days of silken garments and fine trousers, of sweet meats and full bellies, together with my betrayal of the kindness of my father\'s and elder brothers\' teaching, my failure toward the virtue of my teachers\' and friends\' counsels, and my crime of having to this day achieved not one single skill and spent half a lifetime in failure, and to tell it to all the world: my guilt is indeed not to be escaped; but in the inner chambers there were, of themselves, women of note, one after another, and I must by no means, because of my own unworthiness, and in shielding my own shortcomings, cause them all to be blotted out. Though today I have thatch for rafters and mugwort for windows, an earthen stove and a rope bed, yet the morning and evening wind and dew, the willows by the steps and the flowers in the courtyard, have in no way hindered my breast and my pen. And though I am unlearned, and my pen has no grace, what harm is there in using feigned words and rustic speech to unfold a tale, which may cause the inner chambers to be made known, and may also gladden the eyes of the world and break men\'s sorrow and gloom — is it not fitting?" Hence it is called "Jia Yucun" (Feigned Words, Rustic Speech), and so on.</string>

    <string name="chapter-1-004">The Taoist Kongkong then said to the Stone: "Brother Stone, this tale of yours, by your own account, has something of interest in it, and so you have written it here, wishing to publish it as a marvel to the world. Even if I copied it out and took it away, I fear the world would not love to read it." The Stone laughed and answered: "Why, Master, are you so foolish? If you say there is no dynasty to be verified, then what difficulty would there be if you, Master, simply borrowed the reigns of the Han or the Tang and added them on? But I think that the unofficial histories of all ages have all trodden one and the same path; better that I should not borrow this old set, and so be new and strange and of a different fashion. I do but take the substance and the true feeling of the affair; why need one be so tied and bound to dynasty and date? Moreover, of the common folk of the market-place, there are very few who love to read books of order and governance, while those who love pleasing and amusing idle writings are exceedingly many. The unofficial histories of all ages have either slandered rulers and ministers, or vilified men\'s wives and daughters, with lewdness, cruelty and wickedness past all counting. There is, again, a kind of writing of wind and moon, whose obscenity and foul stench, whose poisonous pen, corrupts men\'s sons and younger brothers beyond all counting. As for books of gifted scholars and lovely maidens, a thousand of them all come out of one and the same set, and even then they can never avoid a taint of lewdness and excess, till the whole page is filled with Pan An, Zijian, Xi Shi, Wenjun — the author merely wishes to write out those two love poems and flowery rhapsodies of his own, and so he feigns the names of a man and a woman, and must needs set a petty villain beside them to stir up trouble in between, just like the clown in a play. And the maids and serving-girls have no sooner opened their mouths than out come nothing but classical particles and tags. So, taken one by one, they are all self-contradictory and utterly unreasonable talk; they are not to be compared with these few women whom I have seen with my own eyes and heard with my own ears through half a lifetime. Though I dare not say they are better than all the people in the books of former ages, yet their deeds and the whole story of them may serve to dispel sorrow and break gloom, and there are also a few crooked verses and familiar sayings that may make one spurt out one\'s rice and serve for wine. As for partings and meetings, grief and joy, rise and fall and the fortunes of the hour, these I have followed and tracked, not daring to add the least carving, and have not, merely to please men\'s eyes, on the contrary lost the true tradition. Men of today: the poor are daily weighed down by food and clothing; the rich again harbour a heart of insufficiency; and even if for a moment they have a little leisure, they have again the affairs of greed and lust, of loving beauty and seeking out sorrow — where would they find the leisure to read those books of order and governance?     <string name="chapter-1-014">Mean rooms and empty halls, where once the tablets of office filled the couch; withered grass and dead willows, where once were the scenes of song and dance. Spider-silk fills the carved beams; the green gauze is now pasted on the thatch windows. What matters that the rouge is thick and the powder sweet? How soon are the two temples turned to frost! Yesterday the white bones were seen off at the mound of yellow earth; tonight, beneath the red lamp, in the bed-curtains, lie the mandarin ducks. Gold fills the chests, silver fills the chests, and in the twinkling of an eye a beggar is reviled by all. While he sighs that another\'s life is short, who knows that he himself will not return alive? A good training is no surety that he will not in after days turn a robber. He picks out the fat and the fine; who would have looked for him to fall among the lanes of the flowers? Because he thinks his gauze cap too small, he brings the cangue upon his neck; yesterday he pitied the cold in a ragged jacket, today he complains that the purple python robe is too long. In noisy confusion, one has scarce left the stage before the other comes on; they mistake a foreign land for their own home. How absurd! In the end it is all but making bridal clothes for other people. When the mad limping Taoist heard it, he clapped his hands and laughed, saying: "Well and truly expounded, well and truly expounded!" Shiyin then said, "Let us be off!" and, snatching the bundle from the Taoist\'s shoulder, he carried it on his back, and never went home at all, but drifted away with the mad Taoist. Thereupon the whole neighbourhood was stirred, and the people told it abroad as a piece of news. When Lady Feng heard the tidings, she wept till she died and came to life again, and could only consult with her father and send men to seek and inquire in every place — but what news could they get? There was no help for it; she must needs lean upon her parents for her daily bread. Fortunately she still had two old maidservants to wait upon her; the three of them, mistress and servants, spent day and night making needlework to sell, to help her father\'s expenses. And though that Feng Su grumbled every day, there was nothing to be done.</string>
