    <string name="chapter-1-001">Here is the opening of the first chapter. The author says of himself: because he had once passed through a season of dream and illusion, he therefore hid away the true record of things, and, borrowing the tale of the "Spiritual Understanding", composed this book, the Story of the Stone. Hence he says, "Zhen Shiyin", and so forth. But what matters, and what persons, are recorded in the book? He says again of himself: "Now, drifting through the dust of the world with nothing accomplished, I suddenly bethought me of all the girls of those days, and, weighing them one by one, I felt that in their conduct and understanding they all stood above me. Why is it that I, a grown man with beard and brows, am in truth not the equal of those women? Shame, in sooth, I have in abundance, but regret is of no avail; a great day of nothing-to-be-done! Therefore I would fain take the days gone by, when I leaned upon the grace of Heaven and the merit of my ancestors, the days of broidered robes and silken breeches, of sweet food and rich fare, when I turned my back on the kindness of my father\'s and brothers\' teaching and betrayed the virtue of my teachers\' and friends\' counsels, so that to this day I have not one skill accomplished and half a lifetime of failure lies to my account, and set them all down in a single volume to tell the world: my guilt, indeed, I cannot escape; yet in the inner chambers there were, from of old, women of worth and name, and on no account, because of my own unworthiness, must I shield my own shortcomings and let their memory perish with them. Though today it is a roof of thatch and a window of mugwort, an earthen stove and a rope-bound bed; though morning and evening the wind and the dew blow, and willows stand by the steps and flowers in the court, yet these in no way hinder my heart and my brush. Though I am unlearned and have no art in writing, what harm if I use idle words and village talk to unfold a tale? It may make known the women of the inner chambers, and may likewise please the eyes of the world and dispel men\'s sorrow; is it not fitting?" Hence he says, "Jia Yucun", and so forth.</string>

    <string name="chapter-1-004">The Taoist of the Void then addressed the stone, saying: "Brother Stone, by your own account there is some relish in this story of yours, and so you have written it down here, wishing to publish it abroad as a marvel of the world. As I see it, in the first place there is no dynasty or date to be verified; in the second place there is no great sage or great loyal statesman in it who orders the court and reforms the manners of the age; there are only a few odd women, some passionate, some foolish, some with a little talent or a small virtue, and not one with the worth and ability of Lady Ban or Lady Cai. Even if I copy it out and carry it off, I fear the world will have no taste for reading it." The stone laughed and replied: "How great is my Master\'s folly! If you say there is no dynasty to be verified, why then, let my Master simply borrow the years of the Han or the Tang and tack them on — what difficulty would there be in that? But I think that all the unofficial histories of the past tread one and the same rut; mine does not borrow that pattern, and is therefore all the more new and distinctive. It seeks only to seize the truth and reason of the matter — why must one be bound hand and foot to dynasty and date? Moreover, of the common folk in the marketplace, few care for books of government and order, while very many love pleasant and idle writings. The unofficial histories of old either slander rulers and ministers or vilify other men\'s wives and daughters, and their lewdness and violence cannot be counted. There is besides a kind of writing on wind and moon, whose obscenity and filth, whose poisonous ink, corrupts the sons of good families — and of these too there is no counting. As for the books of gifted scholars and lovely maidens, a thousand volumes all come out of one mould, and they can never end without some lewdness and excess, so that the pages are full of Pan An and Cao Zijian, of Xi Shi and Zhuo Wenjun — merely because the author wants to display the two love poems and flowery rhapsodies he has written, and so he invents the names of a young man and a young woman, and must needs bring in a petty wretch between them to stir up trouble, exactly like the clown in a play. And when the maids and serving-girls open their mouths, it is all 'thee' and 'thou', 'wherefore' and 'thus', nothing but letters and reason. So, looked at one by one, they are all self-contradictory stuff, utterly at odds with sense and feeling — they cannot compare with these few girls whom I have seen with my own eyes and heard with my own ears for half a lifetime. Though I dare not claim that they surpass all the people in the books of former ages, yet the ins and outs of their doings can serve to dispel care and break dulness, and there are a few doggerel verses and familiar sayings in them that may make a man spit out his rice and serve for wine. As for the partings and meetings, the griefs and joys, the rise and fall and the chances of the world, these I follow and track, never daring to add a jot of invention, lest, merely to please men\'s eyes, I should lose the true tradition of them. People today: the poor are daily burdened with food and clothing, the rich are again full of discontent; even if for a moment they have leisure, there is greed of lust and love of beauty, love of goods and the seeking of sorrow — where would they find the time to read those books of government and order? So with this story of mine, I do not wish the world to call it wonderful or say it is marvellous, nor do I insist that the world read it with delight; I only wish that when they are drunk with wine or sated with sleep, or when they would shun the world and escape their grief, they might take it up and play with it — would that not save some of their life and strength? Compared with scheming after vanities and chasing after falsehoods, it would also spare them the harm of gossip and quarrels and the toil of running about on their legs. Moreover, it will give the world new eyes, unlike those old manuscripts of scribblings pulled this way and that, of sudden partings and sudden meetings, whose pages are full of gifted scholars and gentle maidens — of Cao Zijian and Zhuo Wenjun, of Hongniang and Xiaoyu — and all the common worn-out stuff.     <string name="chapter-1-014">"Mean rooms and empty halls — once the court tablets filled the bed; withered grass and dead willows — here once were grounds of song and dance. Spider\'s webs fill the carved beams, and green silk is now pasted over the matted window. What avails it to say that rouge is fresh and powder fragrant, when the hair on both temples has turned to frost? Yesterday, at the earthen mound, they saw off the white bones; tonight, beneath the red lantern, in the bed lie the mandarin ducks. Gold fills the chest, silver fills the chest — in the twinkling of an eye he is a beggar and all men revile him. While he sighs that other men\'s lives are short, who knows that he himself will not come home to his own death! Though sons are trained in the right way, there is no surety that they will not turn to banditry hereafter; and though one choose a rich and noble match, who could expect that she would end in the alleys of flowers? Because a gauze cap seemed too small, one comes to wear the cangue and the chain; yesterday one pitied the cold of a ragged jacket, today one complains that the purple robes are too long: in wild confusion, one man\'s song has scarcely ended when another mounts the stage, and men mistake a foreign land for their own home. How absurd! In the end, it is all but making a wedding garment for another!" When the mad lame Taoist heard it, he clapped his hands and laughed, saying: "An apt interpretation, an apt interpretation!" Shiyin then said, "Let us be off!" He snatched the pilgrim\'s bag from the Taoist\'s shoulder and slung it on his own back, and without going home at all he went off, floating and soaring away with the mad Taoist. At once the whole neighbourhood was in a stir, and everyone took it up as a piece of news to tell. When Madam Feng heard these tidings, she wept till she was beside herself with grief, and could only take counsel with her father and send men everywhere to search for them; but what tidings could they find? There was nothing to be done, and she must needs live out her days leaning upon her parents. Luckily she still had two old maids to serve her, and the three of them, mistress and servants, sewed and stitched day and night and sold their work to help her father\'s expenses. Though Feng Su grumbled every day, there was nothing he could do about it, either.</string>
