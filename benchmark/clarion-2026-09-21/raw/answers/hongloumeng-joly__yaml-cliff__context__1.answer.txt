      This is the opening chapter of the book. The author says of himself: Having once passed through a dream of illusion, he therefore concealed the true facts, and, borrowing the tale of the 'spiritual understanding,' composed this book called 'The Story of the Stone.' Hence it is that he speaks of 'Zhen Shiyin' and so forth. But what matters and what persons are recorded in the book? He says again of himself: 'Now, in the dusty toil of the world, having accomplished nothing, I suddenly bethought me of all the women of former days, and, examining them one by one with care, found that in conduct and understanding they were all above me. Why is it that I, a full-grown man with beard and brows, should verily be inferior to those maidens? Truly my shame is more than enough, and repentance is of no avail, on this day when there is no help for it! In this plight I would fain take the days gone by, when I relied on Heaven's grace and my ancestors' virtue, the days of broidered garments and silken trousers, when I was glutted with sweet things and cloyed with rich fare, and when I turned my back upon the kindness of my father's and brothers' teaching and betrayed the virtue of my teachers' and friends' admonitions, and to the guilt of having now no single art mastered and half a life spent in failure, set them all down in one volume to tell the world: my guilt, indeed, I cannot escape; but in the inner apartments there have, from first to last, been women of worth, and by no means may I, because of my own unworthiness, shield my own shortcomings and cause them all to be lost and forgotten. Though today it be a hut of thatch and a window of wild grass, a tile stove and a rope bed, yet the wind and dew of morning and evening, the willows by the steps and the flowers in the court, do not hinder the free play of my heart and my brush. Though I have no learning and, when I take up my pen, no eloquence, what does it matter if I use feigned words and village talk to unfold and set forth a tale, which may make the women of the inner apartments known to the world, and may also please the eyes of men and break through their gloom and melancholy? Is it not fitting?' Hence it is that he speaks of 'Jia Yucun' and so forth.

      It came about that when the goddess Nüwa smelted stones to repair the heavens, she refined, at the Great Desolate Mountain and the Unfounded Cliff, thirty-six thousand five hundred and one blocks of stubborn stone, twelve chang in height and twenty-four chang square. The goddess Nüwa used only thirty-six thousand five hundred of them, leaving one single block unused, which she cast away at the foot of the Qinggeng Peak of this mountain. Who would have thought that this stone, after being smelted and refined, had already attained spiritual understanding; and seeing that all the other stones had been found fit to repair the heavens, while it alone was of no worth and unfit to be chosen, it fell to grieving over itself and sighing, and day and night cried aloud in shame and vexation.

      The Taoist of the Void accordingly said to the stone: 'Brother Stone, this tale of yours, by your own account, has somewhat of interest in it, and therefore you have written it out here, wishing to publish it to the world as a wondrous tale. In my view, however, in the first place there is no dynasty or date to be verified; and in the second, there is no great worth nor great loyalty, no good government to order the court and reform manners and customs; there are in it nothing but a few unusual women, some passionate, some foolish, some of small talent and slight virtue, and there is no virtue or ability such as a Ban Zhao or a Cai Yan had. Even if I copied it out and took it away, I fear the world would not care to read it.' The stone laughed and answered: 'How foolish my master is! If you say there is no dynasty to be verified, what difficulty is there for my master now to borrow the reign-titles of the Han or the Tang and so patch it up? But I think that the unofficial histories of all ages have all trod one and the same track, and it is better that mine should not borrow this set pattern, but should be novel and distinctive instead, taking only the circumstances and the reasonableness of the thing; why, then, must one be so rigidly bound by dynasty and date? Moreover, among the common folk of the market-place, those who love to read books of government and order are very few, while those who love pleasant and idle writing are exceedingly many. The unofficial histories of the past have either slandered rulers and ministers, or vilified men's wives and daughters, and their lewdness and wickedness are past counting. There is, further, a kind of amorous writing, filthy and foul, a poisonous pen that corrupts young folk — of these, too, there is no end. As for books of fair ladies and gifted scholars, a thousand volumes all come out of one mould, and in these one cannot in the end avoid lewdness and excess, so that the page is filled with Pan An and Zijian, Xi Shi and Wenjun, merely because the author wishes to display his own two love-poems and flowery rhapsodies; thus he invents the names of a man and a woman, and must needs bring in beside them some petty fellow to stir up trouble between them, just like the clown on the stage. And the maids and serving-girls, as soon as they open their mouths, speak in yea and verily, in nothing but letters and reason. So, looked at one by one, they are all self-contradictory and quite contrary to reason and feeling; they cannot compare with these few women whom I have seen with my own eyes and heard with my own ears for half a lifetime. Though I dare not say that they are better than all the people in the books of former ages, yet the whole course of their doings may serve to dispel sorrow and break through melancholy, and there are a few doggerel verses and familiar sayings that may furnish laughter and serve for wine. As for partings and meetings, sorrow and joy, rise and decline, and the fortunes of life, these I have traced and followed step by step, not daring to add the least invention, and so, merely to please men's eyes, lose the true tradition. As for the men of today, the poor are daily burdened by food and clothing, and the rich again harbour a heart of discontent; even if for a moment they have a little leisure, there are still the affairs of lewd desire and lust for beauty, of coveting goods and seeking amusement; where would they find the leisure to read those books of government and order? Therefore this tale of mine neither wishes the world to call it wonderful and marvellous, nor insists that the world take delight in reading it; it wishes only that when they are drunken with lust and lying down full-fed, or when they would shun the world and escape their sorrow, they may take it up and play with it — will it not then save them somewhat of their span of life and their strength? Compared with scheming after vanities and chasing illusions, it will also save them the harm of words and quarrels and the toil of running about on their legs. Moreover, it may give the world new eyes, and it is not like those old drafts that drag one thing after another by the head, that part and meet by fits and starts, whose pages are full of men of talent and virtuous maidens, of Zijian and Wenjun, Hongniang and Xiaoyu, and all the rest of the common hackneyed stuff. What does my master think of it?'

      When the Taoist of the Void heard these words, he pondered for a long while, and once more examined and perused the 'Story of the Stone' from beginning to end. He saw that, although there were in it some passages pointing out villainy, reproving sycophancy, condemning evil and executing wickedness, they were not meant to wound the times or revile the world; and where it touched on the sovereign's benevolence, the minister's goodness, the father's affection and the son's filial piety — wherever the bonds of human relationship were concerned — all was praise of merit and eulogy of virtue, deeply attached and without end, truly not to be compared with other books. Though the main drift of its discourse was but a faithful record of facts, it was not to be compared with feigned and baseless inventions, with a mere round of lewd invitations and wanton assignations, of secret pledges and stolen trysts. And because it had no concern whatever with the times and the world, he copied it out from beginning to end and carried it away, to publish it to the world as a wondrous tale. From this time forth the Taoist of the Void, because he saw form in the void, from form begot feeling, transmitted feeling into form, and from form awakened to the void, changed his name to the Passionate Monk, and changed the 'Story of the Stone' into the 'Record of the Passionate Monk.'       In those days the earth sank away in the south-east, and in this south-eastern corner there was a place called Gusu, in which stood a city named Changmen, the very first or second of the rich and fashionable spots within the red dust. Outside this Changmen there was a Ten-Li Street; within the street was a Benevolence-and-Purity Lane; within the lane was an ancient temple, which, because the ground was narrow and cramped, men all called the Gourd Temple. Beside the temple lived a country gentleman of the surname Zhen, whose name was Fei and whose courtesy-name was Shiyin. His wife by direct marriage was of the Feng family, virtuous and gentle in disposition, and deeply versed in propriety and right. Though the household was not very rich or noble, yet in that district he was reckoned among the leading families. And because this Zhen Shiyin was of a placid and tranquil nature, taking no thought of rank or fame, and found his daily pleasure only in gazing at flowers and tending bamboos, in pouring wine and chanting poems, he was, in truth, a man of the immortal order. There was but one thing wanting: he was now half a hundred years old, and had no son at his knee, only one daughter, whose infant name was Yinglian, and who was just three years of age.

      Mean chambers and empty halls, where once the tablets of office filled the bed; withered grass and dead willows, that once were a place of song and dance. Cobwebs are woven thick upon the carved beams, and green silk is now pasted on the window of tangled weeds. Talk not of rouge so rich and powder so fragrant — how soon the hair at the temples turns to frost! Yesterday, on the mound of yellow earth, the white bones were seen away; tonight, beneath the red lamp and the bed-curtain, love-birds lie. Gold fills the chest, silver fills the chest — in the twinkling of an eye the beggar is reviled by all. Even while sighing that another's life is short, who knows that his own end has come? Though you train your sons aright, you cannot be sure they will not turn robbers in after days. You choose a wealthy match, and who would have looked for her to fall into the streets of flowers? Because a gauze cap was thought too small, the cangue and the chains are brought upon him; yesterday he pitied the cold of a ragged coat, today he complains that the purple python robe is too long. In noisy confusion, you have no sooner finished singing than I come upon the stage; and men mistake a strange land for their own. What utter folly! In the end, all is but making bridal garments for other folk. When the mad, lame Taoist heard it, he clapped his hands and laughed: 'Aptly expounded, aptly expounded!' Shiyin then said: 'Come, let us be off!' He seized the wallet from the Taoist's shoulder and carried it on his own back, and, without going home at all, went off floating in the air with the mad Taoist. This at once stirred the whole neighbourhood, and everyone talked of it as a piece of news. When the Lady Feng heard the tidings, she wept herself into unconsciousness and back to life again, and could do nothing but consult with her father and send people everywhere to search for him — but what news could they find? There was no help for it; she had no choice but to depend on her parents for her daily living. Fortunately she still had two old maidservants to wait upon her, and mistress and servants, three in all, worked needlework day and night and sold it, to help her father with his expenses. And though this Feng Su grumbled every day, there was nothing he could do about it.
