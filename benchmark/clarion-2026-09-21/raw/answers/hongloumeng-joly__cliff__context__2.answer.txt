The author himself says: because he had once passed through a dream and an illusion, he therefore concealed the true events, and, borrowing the tale of \"spiritual understanding,\" wrote this book, called the Story of the Stone. He says again of himself: \"Now, in the dust and toil of the world, having accomplished nothing, I suddenly bethought me of all the women of former days, and, examining them one by one, found that in conduct and understanding they were all above me. How is it that I, a full-grown man, am in truth no match for those women? Truly, shame is more than I can bear, and repentance brings no profit — days of utter helplessness! Whereupon I wished to set down the days when I relied on Heaven's grace and my ancestors' virtue, when I wore brocade and silk, and feasted on sweet and rich fare, and how I turned my back on the kindness of my father's and elder brother's teaching, and failed the virtue of my teachers' and friends' counsel, and how to this day I have mastered no single skill and spent half my life in failure — to compile a volume and tell the world: my guilt is indeed beyond escape; yet in the women's chambers there have always, plainly, been persons of worth, and by no means may I, for my own unworthiness, shield my own shortcomings and cause them all to be blotted out. Though I have no learning, and my pen has no grace, what hindrance is there if I use borrowed words and rustic speech to spin out a story? It may spread the fame of the women's chambers, and may also please the eyes of the world and dispel men's sorrow; is that not fitting?\" Hence the name \"Jia Yucun\" [false words preserved], and so forth."

target: "The Taoist of the Void then addressed the stone, saying: \"Brother Stone, this tale of yours — by your own account it has somewhat of interest in it, and for that reason you have written it here, wishing to put it before the world as a marvel. It is but taking the nature and the reason of the matter — why must one be so rigidly bound to dynasty and date? As for partings and meetings, joys and sorrows, prosperity and decline, I have followed every trace and step, and dared not add the least embellishment, lest for the sake of pleasing men's eyes I should lose the true record. As for the men of today: the poor are daily burdened with food and clothing; the rich again cherish hearts of discontent; and even if for a moment they have leisure, there are lewdness and lust, greed for goods and the seeking of trouble — where would they find the leisure to read books of government and order?
