    <string name="chapter-1-001">This is the opening, the first chapter of the book. The author himself says: because he had once passed through a dream and a vision, he therefore hid away the true facts, and, borrowing the legend of the \"Spiritual Stone,\" wrote this book, The Story of the Stone. Hence the name \"Chen Shih-yin\" (True Facts Concealed), and so on. But what events and what persons does the book record? He says further of himself: \"Now, toiling away in the dust of the world with nothing accomplished, I suddenly thought of all the girls of those days, and, weighing them one by one, I felt that their conduct and understanding were all above my own. Why, then, a full-grown man that I am, should I truly be no match for those women? Truly, my shame is more than enough, and my regret is of no avail — these are days of utter helplessness! At such a time, I wished to set down in a single volume the days when I still depended on Heaven\'s grace and my ancestors\' virtue, the time of brocaded robes and silken trousers, the days of sweet and rich fare; my betrayal of the kindness of my father\'s and elder brothers\' teaching, my failure in the virtue of my teachers\' and friends\' counsels; and my guilt, down to this day, of having mastered not one skill and of a half-life spent in failure — to tell it all to the world. My guilt, indeed, I cannot escape; yet in the inner apartments there have always, of themselves, been worthy women, and by no means must I, because of my own unworthiness, shield my own shortcomings and let them all be blotted out. Though today I have thatch for rafters and brushwood for windows, a clay stove and a rope bed; though morning and evening bring wind and dew, willows by the steps and flowers in the court — none of these need hinder the feelings in my breast or my brush. Though I am unlearned and have no grace of style, what harm is there in using false words and rustic speech to spin out a tale? Thus may the women of the inner apartments be made known and their memory spread abroad, and it may also gladden the eyes of the world and dispel men\'s cares — is that not fitting?\" Hence the name \"Chia Yü-ts\'un\" (Rustic Words), and so on.</string>

If you say there is no dynasty by which it can be verified, what difficulty would there be for my master to borrow the reign-titles of the Han or the T\'ang and tack them on? But I think that all the unofficial histories of the past have trodden one and the same rut; it is better that mine does not borrow that old formula, for then it is all the more novel and distinctive. It is enough that it takes the circumstances and the reason of the matter; why must one be so bound by dynasty and date? Moreover, among the common folk of the marketplace, few care to read books of government and order, while very many love idle writings of pleasant interest. The unofficial histories of the past either slander rulers and ministers or vilify other men\'s wives and daughters, and of lewdness and wickedness there is no counting.     <string name="chapter-1-014">Mean rooms and empty halls, where once the tablets of office filled the couch; withered grass and dead willows, where once was a place of song and dance. Cobwebs hang thick on the carved beams; the green gauze is now pasted over the matted window. What avails it that the rouge is fresh and the powder fragrant, when the hair at the temples has turned to frost? Yesterday, on the mound of yellow earth, white bones were borne away; tonight, beneath the red lamp, beneath the bed-curtain, lie mandarin ducks. Gold fills the chest, silver fills the chest; in the twinkling of an eye a beggar whom all revile. Even as one sighs that another\'s life is short, who knows that one\'s own end has come! Though one trains one\'s children well, who can be sure they will not one day turn to brigandage? Though one chooses the finest grain and the richest fare, who would expect to fall at last into the houses of pleasure? Because a gauze cap seemed too small, one comes to wear the cangue and the chains; yesterday one pitied the cold of a tattered jacket, today one complains that the purple python robe is too long. In the noisy hubbub, you have no sooner finished singing than I come on stage; and one mistakes a foreign land for one\'s own home. How absurd! In the end it is all but making a wedding dress for someone else! When the mad, lame Taoist heard this, he clapped his hands and laughed: \"Well expounded, well expounded!\" Then Shih-yin said, \"Let us be off!\" He snatched the pilgrim\'s bag from the Taoist\'s shoulder and slung it on his own back, and without going home at all he went floating away with the mad Taoist. At once the whole neighbourhood was in a stir, and everyone spread it about as a piece of news. When Madam Feng heard the tidings, she wept herself dead and alive again; there was nothing for it but to consult with her father and send men to search everywhere — but what trace of him could they find? There was no help for it; she could not but depend on her parents for her daily bread. Fortunately, two of her old maidservants were still with her to wait on her, and mistress and servants, the three of them, worked day and night at needlework to sell, helping to meet her father\'s expenses. And though that Feng Su grumbled every day, there was nothing he could do.</string>
