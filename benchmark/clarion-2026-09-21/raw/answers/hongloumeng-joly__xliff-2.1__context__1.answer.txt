          <target>This is the first chapter of the opening volume. The author himself says: Having experienced a dreamlike illusion, he concealed the true events and, borrowing the tale of "spiritual understanding," wrote this book entitled The Story of the Stone. Hence the name "Zhen Shiyin." But what matters and what persons are recorded in the book? He also says: "Now, in the dusty world, busy and achieving nothing, I suddenly think of all the women of former days, and examining them one by one, I find their conduct and understanding all superior to mine. Why am I, a man, so inferior to these women? Truly, shame is more than abundance, and regret is useless on this day of utter helplessness! At this time, I wish to recount the days when I relied on heavenly grace and ancestral virtue, wearing brocade and silk, eating sweet and rich food, betraying the kindness of my father and elder brother's education, failing the virtue of my teachers and friends' guidance, and now, having no skill and half a life in poverty—I compile this volume to inform the world: my guilt is indeed unavoidable, but in the women's quarters there have always been outstanding people, and I must not, because of my unworthiness, protect my own shortcomings and let them all be obliterated. Though today I have thatched eaves and rustic windows, a tile stove and rope bed, the morning and evening wind and dew, the willows by the steps and flowers in the courtyard, do not hinder my feelings and brush. Though I am unlearned and my writing lacks elegance, what harm is there in using false words and rustic speech to elaborate a story, which may also make known the women's quarters and delight the eyes of the world, dispel people's sorrows—is it not fitting?" Hence the name "Jia Yucun."</target>

          <target>The Daoist Kongkong then addressed the stone: "Brother Stone, this story of yours, by your own account, has some interest, and so it is written here, intending to be published as a marvelous tale. In my view, first, there is no dynastic era to verify; second, there is no great virtue or loyalty in governing the court and regulating customs; among them are only a few unusual women, some passionate, some foolish, some with small talents and slight virtues, and none with the virtue and ability of Ban Gu or Cai Yan. Even if I copy it out, I fear the world will not care to read it." The stone laughed and replied: "My master, how foolish you are! If you say there is no dynastic era to verify, what difficulty is there now for you to borrow the reign titles of Han or Tang and add them? But I think that all the unofficial histories of the past follow the same rut. It would be better if mine does not borrow this formula, and instead is novel and distinctive, merely taking the essential truth of the matter. Why must you insist on a dynastic era! Moreover, among the common people of the marketplace, few enjoy reading books of governance, while many love amusing and idle writings. The unofficial histories of the past either slander rulers and ministers, or demean people's wives and daughters; their obscenity and wickedness are beyond count. There is also a kind of romantic writing, whose filth and stench, poisonous to the brush, corrupt the young, again beyond count. As for the books of beauties and geniuses, they all come out of the same mold in a thousand volumes, and they cannot avoid being lewd and excessive, so that the pages are filled with Pan An, Cao Zhi, Xi Shi, Zhuo Wenjun. It is merely that the author wants to write out his own two love poems and flowery rhapsodies, and so invents the names of a man and a woman, and must bring in a villain to stir up trouble between them, like the clown in a play. And the maids and servant girls, when they open their mouths, speak only in classical phrases, either literary or philosophical. So, looking at them one by one, they are all self-contradictory and utterly unreasonable. They are not as good as these few women I have personally seen and heard of in half my life. Though I dare not say they are superior to all the people in earlier books, yet the origin and details of their deeds can also dispel sorrow and break melancholy, and there are a few poor poems and familiar sayings that can make one laugh and serve as wine. As for partings and reunions, joys and sorrows, rises and falls, I have followed the traces and dared not add any fabrication, merely for the sake of people's eyes, which would instead lose the true record. People today—the poor are daily burdened by food and clothing, the rich harbor discontent. Even if they have a little leisure, they have the affairs of lust and greed for beauty, love of goods and seeking sorrow—where would they find time to read those books of governance? Therefore, I do not wish the world to marvel at this story of mine, nor do I insist that the world joyfully read it. I only wish that when they are drunk and satiated in bed, or when avoiding the world and fleeing sorrow, they might take it up for amusement—would it not save some life and energy? Compared to pursuing falsehood and chasing illusions, it also saves the harm of gossip and the toil of running about. Moreover, it may also give the world new eyes, unlike those old manuscripts with their wild connections and random encounters, full of beauties and talented ladies, Cao Zhi, Zhuo Wenjun, Hongniang, Xiaoyu, and all the common clichés. What does my master think of it?"</target>
