The author himself says that, having once passed through a dream and an illusion, he therefore hid away the true facts, and, borrowing the tale of the 'Spiritual Understanding', wrote this book, The Story of the Stone. Hence he speaks of 'Zhen Shiyin' (the True Facts Concealed), and so forth. But what events and what persons are recorded in the book? Of himself he further says: 'Now, as I toil and moil in the dust of the world, having accomplished nothing, I suddenly call to mind all the women of former days, and, weighing them one by one with care, I find that in conduct and in understanding they all stand above me. Why is it that I, a full-grown man with beard and brows, should truly be no match for these women? Truly, shame is more than I can bear, and regret is of no avail, on this day of utter helplessness! In such a pass I would myself set down how I once leaned upon the grace of Heaven and the virtue of my ancestors, the days of brocade and fine silk, of sweet fare and rich meats, and how I turned my back upon the kindness of my father's and elder brothers' teaching and failed the virtue of my teachers' and friends' counsel, so that today I have not a single skill to my name and, for half a lifetime, the guilt of failure — to set it down in one volume and make it known to all men under Heaven: my guilt is indeed not to be escaped, but in the women's chambers there have always been, plainly and truly, those who were somebody, and by no means may they all be blotted out because of my worthlessness and my covering of my own shortcomings. Though today it be but thatched rafters and matted windows, a tile stove and a rope bed, yet the wind and dew of morning and evening, the willows by the steps and the flowers in the courtyard, have nothing in them to hinder my heart or my brush. Though I have no learning and no grace of style in my writing, what harm is there if I use borrowed words and village speech to act out and set forth a story? It may make the women's chambers shine forth and be handed down, and it may likewise please the eyes of the world and break through men's gloom — would that not be fitting?' Hence he speaks of 'Jia Yucun' (Words of the Village), and so forth."

Though half of them have already fallen into the dust, they are not yet all assembled.' The Daoist said: 'If that be so, then let us go with you.'"

msgstr "Mean rooms and empty halls, where once the tablets of office lay scattered on the couch; withered grass and sere willows, where once were the grounds of song and dance. The spider's web hangs thick upon the carved beams, and the green silk gauze is now pasted over the brushwood window. Talk not of paint still fresh and powder still sweet — how soon the hair on either temple is turned to frost! Yesterday, on the yellow earth mound, they bore away white bones; tonight, beneath the red lantern, the love-birds lie together in the bed. Gold fills the chest and silver fills the chest — in the twinkling of an eye a beggar, and all men revile him. You sigh that another's life is short, and little know that when you go home it is your own death! Though you teach with all correctness, it is not certain your son will not turn brigand in after days. Though you choose a mate of noble house, who would have looked for her to fall into the streets of flowers? Because the gauze cap seemed too small, one brings the cangue and the chains upon oneself; yesterday one pitied the cold of a tattered coat, today one grumbles that the purple python robe is too long. In the noisy bustle, your song is no sooner done than I come on the stage, and one takes an alien land for one's own home. How absurd it all is — in the end, everything is but the making of wedding garments for other folk! When the mad and lame Daoist heard this, he clapped his hands and laughed, saying: 'A fitting interpretation, a fitting interpretation!' Shiyin then said, 'Let us be off!' and, snatching the bundle from the Daoist's shoulder, he shouldered it and went straightway off, never going home, but floating away together with the mad Daoist. Presently the whole neighbourhood was in a stir, and everyone told it about as a piece of news. When Mrs. Feng heard of it, she wept herself into and out of death, and could only take counsel with her father, sending people everywhere to search for him — but how could any word be found? There was nothing to be done; she had no choice but to rely upon her parents for her daily bread. Happily there were still two old maidservants at her side to wait upon her, and mistress and servants, three in all, worked at needlework day and night to sell, helping to meet her father's expenses. And though that Feng Su grumbled every day, there was nothing he could do about it either."
