msgstr "This is the opening chapter of the book. The author says of himself: Having once passed through a dream of illusion, he concealed the true facts, and borrowing the tale of the 'Spiritual Stone', wrote this book called The Story of the Stone. Hence the name Zhen Shiyin, and so on. But what events and what persons are recorded in the book? He further says: 'Now, in the dusty world, busy and toiling, having accomplished nothing, I suddenly recall all the women of former days. Examining them one by one, I feel that their conduct and understanding all surpass mine. How is it that I, a full-grown man, am truly not the equal of those women? Truly I have shame in abundance, and regret that is useless—a day of utter helplessness! At this point, I wish to set down in a volume the past days when I relied on Heaven's grace and my ancestors' virtue, when I wore brocade and fine silk, when I ate sweet and rich food to the full, while betraying the grace of my father's and elder brothers' instruction and failing the virtue of my teachers' and friends' admonitions—and so to the crime of today: no skill accomplished, half a lifetime in poverty and failure—and tell it to all under heaven: my guilt is indeed unavoidable, but in the women's chambers there have always been worthy persons. I must by no means, because of my own unworthiness, shield my shortcomings and cause them all to be obliterated. Though today I have thatched rafters and a door of weeds, a tile stove and a rope bed, the morning and evening wind and dew, the willows by the steps and the flowers in the courtyard, still do not hinder my feelings and my brush. Though I am unlearned and my writing lacks elegance, what harm is there in using fictitious rustic talk to unfold a story? It may make the women's chambers known to posterity, and it may also please the eyes of the world and dispel people's sorrow and gloom. Is it not fitting?' Hence the name Jia Yucun, and so on."

msgstr "The Daoist of Vacuous Space then said to the stone: 'Brother Stone, this story of yours—by your own account it has some interest, and so you wrote it here, wishing to publish a marvel to the world. It contains only a few unusual women, some passionate, some foolish, some with small talents and slight virtues, and none of the virtue or ability of Ban Zhao or Cai Wenji. Even if I copied it and took it out, I fear the world would not care to read it.' The stone laughed and replied: 'Why is my master so foolish? If you say there is no dynasty to verify, what difficulty would there be for my master to borrow the reign names of the Han or Tang and add them in? But I think that all the unofficial histories of past ages follow the same rut. It would be better if mine did not borrow that formula; on the contrary, it would be fresh and distinctive. It merely takes the facts and reason of the matter, so why must it be bound to dynasty and date? Moreover, among the common people of the marketplace, few like to read books on government and order, while many love playful and entertaining writing. The unofficial histories of the past either slander rulers and ministers, or degrade other men's wives and daughters; their lewdness and ferocity are too many to count. There is another kind of wind-and-moon writing, obscene and foul, poisonous to the brush, ruining young people, also too many to count. As for books of beauties and talents, a thousand volumes all come from one mold, and in the end they cannot avoid lewd excess, so that the page is full of Pan An, Zijian, Xi Shi, and Wenjun; it is merely that the author wants to write out his own two love poems and flowery rhapsodies, and so fabricates the names of a man and a woman, and must also bring in a petty villain to stir up trouble between them, just like the clown in a play. And the maids and servant girls open their mouths with classical phrases; if it is not literary, it is moral. So, looking at them one by one, they are all self-contradictory and quite unreasonable. They are not as good as these few women whom I have seen and heard with my own eyes and ears for half a lifetime. Though I dare not say they are stronger than all the people in earlier books, yet their story, root and branch, can dispel sorrow and break melancholy, and there are a few rustic poems and familiar sayings that can make one spurt rice and accompany wine. As for partings and meetings, sorrow and joy, rise and fall, and fortunes met, I have followed their traces and footsteps, not daring to add the least fabrication, merely to please people's eyes and thereby lose the true record. People today—the poor are daily burdened by food and clothing, the rich cherish hearts of insufficiency; even if they have a little leisure, there are again lust and greed for beauty, love of goods and seeking of sorrow. Where would they find time to read books on government and order? Therefore, this story of mine does not wish the world to call it wonderful or marvelous, nor does it insist that the world delight in reading it; I only wish that when they are drunk with lust and lying full, or when avoiding the world and fleeing sorrow, they might play with it once. Would it not save them some lifespan and strength? Compared with scheming after emptiness and chasing delusion, it would also save the harm of gossip and quarrels and the busy running of legs and feet. Furthermore, it may let the world change its eyes, unlike those old manuscripts of tangled nonsense, sudden partings and meetings, pages full of talents and fair ladies, Zijian, Wenjun, Hongniang, Xiaoyu, and all the common stale formulas. What does my master think of it?'"

msgstr "Mean rooms and empty halls—in those years, tablets covered the bed; withered grass and dead willows—once they were places of song and dance. Spider silk now fills the carved beams; green gauze is pasted on the thatch window. Why speak of rouge being rich and powder fragrant, when the temples have already turned to frost? Yesterday, at the yellow earth mound, white bones were sent off; tonight, beneath the red lamp and curtain, lovebirds lie. Gold fills the chest, silver fills the chest; in the twinkling of an eye a beggar, and everyone reviles him. Just as you sigh that another's life is short, how do you know that when you return home there is mourning! Though you teach with method, you cannot be sure he will not become a robber in days to come. You choose a rich match, but who would expect her to fall into the pleasure quarter! Because you dislike the gauze cap being small, you end up in the cangue and chains. Yesterday you pitied the cold of a torn coat; today you complain the purple robe is too long. In noisy confusion, you finish your song and I come on stage; we mistake a foreign land for our home. How absurd! In the end all is making wedding clothes for others! When the mad lame Daoist heard this, he clapped his hands and laughed: 'A fitting interpretation, a fitting interpretation!' Shiyin then said, 'Let us go!' He seized the Daoist's cloth bag from his shoulder, slung it on his own back, and did not go home at all, but floated away with the mad Daoist. For a time the neighborhood was stirred, and everyone took it as a piece of news to spread. When the Feng family heard this, she cried herself half dead, and could only consult with her father and send people everywhere to search, but what news did they find? There was nothing to be done; she had no choice but to rely on her parents for her daily living. Fortunately she still had two old maidservants to wait on her. The three, mistress and servants, worked daily at needlework to sell and helped her father with expenses. Although Feng Su complained every day, there was nothing he could do."
