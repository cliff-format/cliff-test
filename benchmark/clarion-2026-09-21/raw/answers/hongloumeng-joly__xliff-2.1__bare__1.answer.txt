          <target state="translated">This is the opening of the first chapter. The author speaks in his own person: having once passed through an illusion of dream, he concealed the truth, and, borrowing the tale of the "spirit's understanding," composed this book, The Story of the Stone. Hence the name "Zhen Shiyin" [True Matters Concealed], as it were. But what events and what persons are recorded in the book? He says again of himself: "Now, toiling away in the dust of the world and having accomplished nothing, I suddenly thought of all the women of former days, and examined them one by one with care, and felt that their conduct and understanding all surpassed my own. Why must I, a full-grown man, be so inferior to these women? Indeed, shame overwhelms me, and regret is useless on this day of utter helplessness! Thereupon I wished to record the times when I relied on the grace of Heaven and the virtue of my ancestors, the days of brocade and silk, of rich food and full fare, my betrayal of my father's and elder brother's instruction, my failure in the kindness of my teachers and friends, and how it has come to this—that I have mastered not a single skill and have spent half my life in failure—and to compile it into a volume to tell the world: my guilt is indeed not to be escaped, yet the women's quarters have always contained many notable persons, and I must not, because of my own unworthiness, shield my own shortcomings so as to blot them all out. Though today I dwell amid thatched eaves and brushwood windows, an earthen stove and a rope bed, the morning and evening wind and dew, the willows by the steps and the flowers in the court, in no way hinder my breast and my brush. Though I am without learning, and my pen lacks elegance, what harm is there in using feigned words and rustic speech to unfold a story? It may make the women's quarters known and celebrated, and may also delight the eyes of the world and dispel men's sorrow—is it not fitting?" Hence the name "Jia Yucun" [Words Preserved in Idle Talk], as it were.</target>

          <target state="translated">The Daoist Kongkong thereupon addressed the stone, saying: "Brother Stone, this tale of yours, by your own account, has something of interest in it, and so you have written it down here, wishing to bring it before the world as a tale of the marvelous. As I see it, in the first place, there is no dynasty or date by which to trace it; in the second place, it contains no great worthies or great loyal ministers who ordered the court and reformed the customs by good government—there are only a few unusual women, some passionate, some foolish, some with small talents and trifling virtues, and none of them possessing the moral power of a Ban Gu or a Cai Yan. Even if I should copy it out and take it away, I fear the world will not care to read it."

The stone laughed and answered: "How foolish you are, Master! If you say there is no dynasty by which to trace it, then surely I might borrow the reign periods of the Han or the Tang and append them—what difficulty would there be? But I think that the wild histories of past ages have all followed a single rut, and it would be better that mine should not borrow this convention, and so be the more novel and distinctive. It is only a matter of taking the essential truth of the events—why must one be so rigidly bound to a dynasty and a date? Moreover, among the vulgar folk of the marketplace, few care to read books of governance and order, while those who love pleasing and amusing idle writings are especially many. The wild histories of past ages either slander rulers and ministers, or vilify men's wives and daughters; their lewdness and cruelty are beyond counting. There is, further, a kind of wind-and-moon writing, obscene and foul, a poisonous pen that corrupts people's sons and daughters—these too are beyond counting. As for the books of beauties and gifted scholars, a thousand volumes are cast in a single mold, and in them one can never avoid licentious excess, so that the page is filled with Pan An, Cao Zijian, Xi Shi, and Zhuo Wenjun. It is merely that the author wishes to display a couple of his own love poems and flowery rhapsodies, and so he invents a man and a woman with surnames and names, and must needs introduce a petty person between them to sow discord, just like the clown in a play. And the maids and serving-girls, no sooner do they open their mouths than out come 'thee' and 'thou,' 'verily' and 'indeed'—nothing but refinement or morality. Thus, looking at them one by one, they are all self-contradictory and grossly improbable. They cannot compare with these few women whom I have seen and heard with my own eyes and ears over half a lifetime. Though I dare not say they surpass all the persons in the books of former ages, yet the circumstances of their affairs can likewise dispel sorrow and break gloom, and there are also a few doggerel verses and familiar sayings that can make one sputter with laughter over wine. As for partings and reunions, joys and sorrows, prosperity and decline, and the turns of fortune—these I have traced and followed with careful steps, and dared not embellish in the least, lest I merely gratify men's eyes and in so doing lose the true record. The people of today: the poor are daily burdened by food and clothing, while the rich are filled with a heart of discontent. Even if for a time they have a little leisure, there are again the matters of lust and lechery, of craving goods and seeking amusement—where would they find the leisure to read those books of governance and order? Therefore I do not wish this tale of mine to be praised by the world as marvelous, nor do I insist that the world read it with delight; I only wish that when they are drunken with lust and lying full-fed, or when they would shun the world and flee their sorrows, they might take it up and amuse themselves with it—would it not thus save them some years of life and strength? It is better than scheming after the empty and chasing the false, for it spares them the harm of words and quarrels and of running about on foot. Moreover, it may open the world's eyes anew; it is not like those old manuscripts that drag things in at random, that meet and part now here, now there, that are filled with pages of gifted scholars and fair maidens—a Cao Zijian and a Zhuo Wenjun, a Hongniang and a Xiaoyu—all the common stock-in-trade. What does my master think of this?"</target>

          <target state="translated">When the Daoist Kongkong heard these words, he pondered for a long while, then went through The Story of the Stone once more.           <target state="translated">One day, in the long daylight of midsummer, Shiyin was sitting at leisure in his study, until his hand grew tired and he put down his book, and leaning on the table he rested a little, and before he knew it fell into a dim sleep. He heard only the Daoist ask: "What do you mean to do with that stupid creature you carry?" The monk laughed and said: "Set your mind at ease. There is now a case of passion that ought to be settled. This whole company of passionate lovers has not yet been reborn into the world. Taking this opportunity, I will smuggle this stupid creature in among them, so that he may go and experience it all." The Daoist said: "So it seems that these passionate sinners are again about to go into the world to undergo their calamity? But I do not know where they will descend." The monk laughed and said: "This is laughable to relate—indeed, it is a rare thing, unheard of through the ages. In time, having received the essence of Heaven and Earth, and again been nourished by rain and dew, it came to shed its grassy womb and woody substance and to take on human form, and it attained the form of a woman. Just now, as it happens, this attendant Shenying let his earthly heart flare up for a moment, and taking advantage of this bright and peaceful age, he wished to descend into the world to undergo the illusions of fate; he has already entered his name before the Goddess of Disenchantment.
