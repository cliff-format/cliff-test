          <target>This is the opening chapter of the book. The author says of himself: because he had once passed through a great illusion, he therefore concealed the true facts, and, borrowing the legend of "Spiritual Understanding," composed this book, The Story of the Stone. Hence the name "Zhen Shiyin" (True Facts Concealed), and so forth. But what events, and what persons, are recorded in the book? He says again of himself: "Now, in the dust and turmoil of the world, with not one thing accomplished, I suddenly bethought me of all the girls of former days, and, weighing them one by one, I felt that in conduct and in understanding they were all above me. Why should I, a full-grown man with beard and brows, in truth be no match for those girls in skirts and hairpins? Shame, indeed, I have in plenty; and regret is of no avail on this day when there is nothing to be done! In such a pass, I would fain set down in one volume all those past days when I leaned upon Heaven's grace and my ancestors' virtue, my seasons of brocade garments and silk trousers, of rich fare and sweet dainties, my betrayal of the kindness of my father and elder brothers in schooling me, my failure to requite the goodness of my teachers and friends in admonishing me, and even the guilt of my present state, in which I have not one skill to my name and half a lifetime has gone to ruin — and make it all known to the men of the world: my own guilt is indeed not to be escaped, but in the inner chambers there were, from the first, women worthy of record, and by no means must my unworthiness, in shielding my own shortcomings, bring them likewise to oblivion. Though today I have thatch for rafters and weeds for windows, an earthen stove and a rope bed, yet the wind and dew of morning and evening, the willows by the steps and the flowers in the court, have not hindered my breast or my brush. And though I have no learning, and no grace of style when I put pen to paper, what harm is there in using feigned words and rustic speech to unfold a tale? It may serve to make the maidens of the inner chambers known to the world, and it may likewise please the eyes of the age and dispel men's sorrow and gloom — is that not fitting?" Hence the name "Jia Yucun" (Feigned Words and Rustic Speech), and so forth.</target>

Who would have thought that this stone, having undergone the refining, had already acquired spiritual understanding; and seeing that all the other stones had been found fit to mend the sky, while it alone was without worth and unfit for selection, it began to murmur and sigh over itself, weeping and wailing day and night in shame and mortification.</target>

          <source>空空道人遂向石頭說道：“石兄，你這一段故事，据你自己說有些趣味 ，故編寫在此，意欲問世傳奇．据我看來，第一件，無朝代年紀可考，第二 件，并無大賢大忠理朝廷治風俗的善政，其中只不過几個异樣女子，或情或 痴，或小才微善，亦無班姑，蔡女之德能．我縱抄去，恐世人不愛看呢。” 石頭笑答道：“我師何太痴耶！若云無朝代可考，今我師竟假借漢唐等年紀 添綴，又有何難？但我想，歷來野史，皆蹈一轍，莫如我這不借此套者，反 倒新奇別致，不過只取其事体情理罷了，又何必拘拘于朝代年紀哉！再者， 市井俗人喜看理治之書者甚少，愛适趣閒文者特多．歷來野史，或訕謗君相 ，或貶人妻女，奸淫凶惡，不可胜數．更有一种風月筆墨，其淫穢污臭，屠 毒筆墨，坏人子弟，又不可胜數．至若佳人才子等書，則又千部共出一套， 且其中終不能不涉于淫濫，以致滿紙潘安，子建，西子，文君，不過作者要 寫出自己的那兩首情詩艷賦來，故假擬出男女二人名姓，又必旁出一小人其 間撥亂，亦如劇中之小丑然．且鬟婢開口即者也之乎，非文即理．故逐一看 去，悉皆自相矛盾，大不近情理之話，竟不如我半世親睹親聞的這几個女子 ，雖不敢說強似前代書中所有之人，但事跡原委，亦可以消愁破悶，也有几 首歪詩熟話，可以噴飯供酒．至若离合悲歡，興衰際遇，則又追蹤躡跡，不 敢稍加穿鑿，徒為供人之目而反失其真傳者．今之人，貧者日為衣食所累， 富者又怀不足之心，縱然一時稍閒，又有貪淫戀色，好貨尋愁之事，那里去 有工夫看那理治之書？所以我這一段故事，也不愿世人稱奇道妙，也不定要 世人喜悅檢讀，只愿他們當那醉淫飽臥之時，或避世去愁之際，把此一玩， 豈不省了些壽命筋力？就比那謀虛逐妄，卻也省了口舌是非之害，腿腳奔忙 之．再者，亦令世人換新眼目，不比那些胡牽亂扯，忽离忽遇，滿紙才人 淑女，子建文君紅娘小玉等通共熟套之舊稿．我師意為何如？”</source>
          <target>Kongkong Daoren (the Taoist of Vacuity) then spoke to the stone: "Brother Stone, this tale of yours — by your own account it has a certain interest, and so you have written it out here, meaning to publish it to the world as a marvel. As I see it, in the first place there is no dynasty or year to give it a date; in the second place there is no great sage or great loyal minister administering the court and reforming the manners of the age by good government; and in it there are only a few uncommon women, some passionate, some foolish, some of small talent or slight virtue, and none with the worth and ability of Ban Gu's aunt or of Cai Wenji. Even if I were to copy it out, I fear the world would not care to read it." The stone laughed and answered: "Why, Teacher, are you so foolish? If you say there is no dynasty to date it, could you not now, Teacher, borrow the years of the Han or the Tang and append them to it — what difficulty would there be? But I think that all the unofficial histories of past ages have trodden one and the same rut, and that mine, which does not borrow that convention, is on the contrary something new and distinctive; it seeks only to take the substance and the reason of the matter, and why should it be tied and bound to dynasty and year? Moreover, of the common folk in the market-place, very few care to read books on government and order, while those who love agreeable and diverting writings are especially many. The unofficial histories of former times either slander rulers and ministers, or degrade men's wives and daughters; their lewdness, cruelty and villainy are beyond all reckoning. There is, besides, a kind of writing of breeze and moonlight, whose filth and stench, whose poison of the brush, corrupts the sons of good families, and that too is beyond all reckoning. As for books of the gifted scholar and the lovely maiden, a thousand of them come out of one and the same mould, and even in these there is in the end no avoiding lewdness and excess, so that the whole page is filled with Pan An, Cao Zijian, Xi Shi and Zhuo Wenjun; the author merely wishes to set down those two love poems and flowery rhapsodies of his own, and therefore invents a man and a woman with names and surnames, and must further bring in some petty fellow to stir up trouble between them, just like the clown in a play. And the serving-maids and slave-girls, the moment they open their mouths, speak nothing but 'thee' and 'thou,' 'shalt' and 'dost' — nothing but letters and philosophy. So, looked at one by one, they are all self-contradictory, and their talk is utterly wide of reason and of feeling. They cannot compare with these few women whom I myself have seen with my own eyes and heard with my own ears in half a lifetime. Though I dare not say that they surpass all the people in the books of former ages, yet their deeds and the whole story of them can dispel care and break gloom, and there are also a few doggerel verses and homely sayings that can make one laugh and cheer the wine. As for meetings and partings, joys and sorrows, rise and fall and the turns of fortune, I have traced and followed them, not daring to add the least patchwork, merely to please men's eyes and so lose the true record. The men of today: the poor are burdened day after day by food and clothing, and the rich again nurse hearts of insufficiency; and even if for a while they have leisure, there are still lust and lechery, greed for goods and the pursuit of cares — where would they find the leisure to read those books of government and order? Therefore, as for this tale of mine, I do not wish the world to call it wonderful or praise it as fine, nor do I insist that the world shall delight in it and read it; I only wish that, when they are drunk with wine and lie down sated with sleep, or when they withdraw from the world to escape their cares, they may take it up for a little play — would that not save them some of their span of life and strength? It is better than scheming after emptiness and chasing after vanities, and it also spares them the mischief of talk and quarrels and the toil of hurrying legs and feet. Moreover, it may give the world a new pair of eyes, and is not to be compared with those old manuscripts that drag and pull at random, part and meet by turns, pages full of talented scholars and virtuous maidens — Cao Zijian and Zhuo Wenjun, Hongniang and Xiaoyu and all the rest of the worn-out commonplaces. What does my Teacher think of it?"</target>
