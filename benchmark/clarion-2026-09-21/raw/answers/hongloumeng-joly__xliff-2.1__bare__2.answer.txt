          <target state="translated">This is the opening chapter of the book. The author says of himself that, having once passed through a dream of illusion, he has therefore hidden away the true facts and, borrowing the tale of the "Spiritual Understanding" stone, has written this book, The Story of the Stone. Hence the name Zhen Shiyin — "Truth Concealed." But what events, and what persons, are recorded in the book? He says again of himself: "In this dusty world of restless toil, having accomplished nothing, I suddenly thought of all the women of my former days and examined them one by one with care, and it seemed to me that their conduct and understanding were all above my own. How is it that I, a full-grown man with beard and brows, should truly fall short of these skirt-and-hairpin women? Truly my shame is more than enough, and regret is of no avail — a day of utter helplessness! And so I wished to set down the days when I still relied upon Heaven's grace and my ancestors' virtue, the time of silken clothes and fine trousers, the days of sweet and rich fare; my turning my back on the kindness of my father and elder brothers' teaching, my betrayal of the virtue of my teachers' and friends' counsels; and, down to today, my guilt of having mastered not one skill and having spent half a life in failure — to set it all down in one volume and tell it to the world: my guilt is indeed not to be escaped, but in the women's apartments there have always been true and shining people, and by no means may I, because of my own unworthiness, shield my own shortcomings and let them all be blotted out as well. Though today I have thatched rafters and a door of wild grass, a tile stove and a rope bed, yet the wind and dew of morning and evening, the willows by the steps and the flowers in the court, have never hindered the feelings in my breast or my brush and ink. Though I have no learning and no elegance when I set brush to paper, what harm is there in using feigned words and rustic speech to unfold a story? It may cause the women's apartments to be made known and handed down, and it may also please the eyes of the world and dispel men's sorrow and gloom — is that not fitting?" Hence the name Jia Yucun — "False Words, Rustic Speech" — and so on.</target>

          <target state="translated">The Taoist Kongkong ("Empty-Empty") then said to the stone: "Brother Stone, this story of yours — by your own account it has some interest in it, and so you have written it out here, meaning to publish it to the world as a wondrous tale. But as I see it, in the first place there is no dynasty or year that can be verified; and in the second place it contains no great worthies or loyal ministers who set the court in order and reformed the manners of the age; there are only a few peculiar women, some passionate, some foolish, some with small talents and slight virtues, and none of them with the worth and ability of a Ban Zhao or a Cai Wenji. Even if I copied it out and took it away, I fear the world would not care to read it."

The stone laughed and answered: "Why is my master so foolish? If you say there is no dynasty to be verified, why should my master not simply borrow the reign-years of the Han or the Tang and tack them on — what difficulty would there be in that? But I think that all the unofficial histories of the past have trodden one and the same path, and that mine, which borrows none of their conventions, is instead new and distinctive. It is only that I take the essential truth and reason of the matter; why must I be bound fast by dynasty and year? Moreover, among the common folk of the market-place, those who like to read books on governance are very few, while those who love pleasing and diverting trifles are exceedingly many. The unofficial histories of the past either slander rulers and ministers or debase men's wives and daughters; lewdness, cruelty and wickedness are beyond counting. There is also a kind of writing of wind and moon whose obscenity and filth, whose poisonous brush, corrupts the young, and this too is beyond counting. As for books of the gifted scholar and the lovely maiden, a thousand volumes all come from one and the same mould, and in them there is in the end no avoiding the lewd and the extravagant, so that the pages are filled with Pan An, Cao Zijian, Xi Shi and Zhuo Wenjun — it is only that the author wants to show off those two love poems and flowery rhapsodies of his own, and so invents a man and a woman with names and surnames, and must needs bring in a petty villain to stir confusion between them, just like the clown in a play. And the maids and serving-girls, the moment they open their mouths, speak in thee and thou and quote the classics — nothing but learning or moralizing. Look at them one by one, and they are all full of self-contradiction, quite beyond reason and sense. They are not to be compared with these few women whom I have seen with my own eyes and heard with my own ears for half my life. I would not presume to say that they surpass all the people in the books of earlier ages, but their deeds and the whole story of them can dispel sorrow and break through gloom, and there are a few doggerel verses and commonplace sayings in them that can make one laugh over a meal or serve for drinking. As for partings and reunions, joys and griefs, risings and failings, I have followed the tracks of the real events and dared not add the slightest embellishment, merely to please men's eyes and so lose the true record. People today — the poor are burdened daily by food and clothing, and the rich are filled with discontent. Even if for a moment they have a little leisure, they have their own greed for pleasure and lust for beauty, their love of goods and their seeking after cares. Where would they find the time to read those books on governance? And so I do not wish the world to call this story of mine a marvel or praise it as wonderful, nor do I insist that the world read it with delight; I only wish that when they are drunk with pleasure and lying full-fed, or when they would escape the world and put off their cares, they might take it up and play with it — would that not spare them a little of their years of life and their strength of limb? Compared with scheming after vanities and chasing after falsehoods, it would also spare them the harm of quarrels and gossip and the toil of running about on their legs. Moreover, it will give the world new eyes, unlike those old manuscripts with their wild pullings and taggings, their sudden partings and sudden meetings, their pages full of talented men and virtuous maidens, of Cao Zijian and Zhuo Wenjun, of Hongniang and Xiaoyu, and every common, stale convention.
