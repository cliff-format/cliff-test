          <target>On the day of the full moon in the fourth month of the second year of Chien Ning, the Emperor held court in Wên Tê Hall. He had but ascended the throne when a violent wind suddenly rose from a corner of the hall, and there appeared a great green snake that flew down from the beam and coiled upon the throne. The Emperor fell in terror; his attendants hurried him into the palace, and all the officials fled for their lives. In a moment the snake had vanished. Then suddenly came great thunder and rain, with hail, which did not cease until midnight and destroyed countless houses. In the second month of the fourth year of Chien Ning, an earthquake shook Loyang; the sea overflowed, and the dwellers along the coast were all swept by great waves into the sea. In the first year of Kuang Ho, hens turned into cocks. On the first day of the sixth month, a black vapor more than ten chang long flew into Wên Tê Hall. In the seventh month of autumn, a rainbow appeared at Yü T'ang; the mountains and cliffs of Wu Yüan all crumbled and split. These various evil omens were not confined to one kind alone.</target>

          <target>The Emperor issued an edict asking the ministers the cause of these calamities. The Court Counselor Ts'ai Yung presented a memorial, holding that the falling of the rainbow and the changing of fowls were brought about by the interference of women and eunuchs in government. His words were exceedingly blunt and upright. The Emperor read the memorial and sighed, then rose to change his garments. Ts'ao Chieh watched him secretly from behind and told all to the attendants. Then he had Ts'ai Yung accused on other grounds and sent back to his fields. Later Chang Jang, Chao Chung, Feng Hsü, Tuan Kuei, Ts'ao Chieh, Hou Lan, Chien Shih, Ch'eng K'uang, Hsia Yün, and Kuo Sheng, ten men, banded together for evil and were called the "Ten Regular Attendants." The Emperor trusted and honored Chang Jang, calling him "Father"; the government daily declined, until the hearts of the people turned toward disorder and robbers rose like swarms.</target>

The old man called Chüeh into a cave and gave him three volumes of a heavenly book, saying: "This is called The Essential Arts of Great Peace.           <target>This man was not very fond of reading; he was of a mild and generous nature, sparing of words, and did not show joy or anger on his face. He had always cherished great ambitions and delighted in befriending heroes of the world. He was seven feet five inches tall, with ears that hung to his shoulders and hands that reached below his knees; his eyes could turn to see his own ears. His face was like a crown of jade, and his lips as if painted with vermilion. He was a descendant of Liu Sheng, Prince Ching of Chung-shan, and a great-great-grandson of Emperor Ching of Han. His surname was Liu, his name Pei, his style Hsüan-tê.
