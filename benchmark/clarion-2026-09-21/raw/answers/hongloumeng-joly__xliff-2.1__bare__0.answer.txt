          <target>This is the opening chapter of the first volume. The author tells us that, having lived through a dream and an illusion, he conceals the true events and, borrowing the tale of "spiritual understanding," writes this book, The Story of the Stone. Hence the name "Zhen Shiyin," and so forth. But what deeds and what people does the book record? The author also says: "In this dusty, bustling world I have accomplished nothing, when suddenly I think of all the girls of former days and, one by one, examine them closely; and I find that their conduct and understanding are all above my own. What a shame it is that I, a full-grown man, should truly fall short of these women! Truly my shame is more than enough, and my regret is of no use — a day of utter helplessness! At such a time I would write down the days of my past, when I relied upon Heaven's grace and my ancestors' virtue, the days of brocade and silk, of rich and dainty fare, and — having betrayed the kindness of my father and brothers' teaching and failed the virtue of my teachers' and friends' guidance, so that today I have mastered no single skill and have spent half a life in failure — compose a volume to tell the world: my guilt, indeed, cannot be escaped, but in the women's quarters there were, all along, one after another of outstanding people, and I must not, because of my own worthlessness, shield my own shortcomings and let them all be blotted out as well. Though today I have thatched rafters and a brushwood window, an earthen stove and a rope-strung bed, still the wind and dew of morning and evening, the willows by the steps and the flowers in the courtyard, do nothing to hinder my heart and my brush. Though I am unlearned and my writing has no grace, what harm is there in using false words and rustic speech to unfold a story? It may make the women's quarters known and handed down, and it may also please the world's eyes and dispel men's sorrows — is that not fitting?" Hence the name "Jia Yucun," and so forth.</target>

          <target>Vanitas the Taoist then said to the Stone: "Brother Stone, this story of yours — by your own account it has some interest in it, and so you have written it here, meaning to publish it as a tale for the world. But as I see it, first, there is no dynasty or date by which it can be verified; second, there is in it no great sage or great loyal minister who brings order to the court and reforms the morals of the age; there are only a few unusual women, some passionate, some foolish, some of small talent and slight virtue, with nothing of the worth or ability of a Ban Zhao or a Cai Wenji. Even if I copied it out, I fear the world would not care to read it." The Stone laughed and answered: "How foolish you are, master! If you say there is no dynasty to be verified, why should you not simply borrow the dates of the Han or the Tang and tack them on — what difficulty would that be? But I think that the unofficial histories of every age have all trodden one and the same rut, and that mine, which does not borrow that pattern, is instead new and distinctive. I take only the essential truth of the matter — why should I be bound hand and foot by dynasty and date? Besides, among the common people of the marketplace there are very few who enjoy books of statecraft and government, while those who love pleasant and amusing writing are especially many. The unofficial histories of the past either slander rulers and ministers, or disparage other men's wives and daughters; lewdness and cruelty are too many to be counted. And there is another kind of writing of wind and moonlight, whose obscenity and filth, whose poisonous strokes, corrupt the sons of good families — that, too, is beyond counting. As for the books of beauties and talented scholars, a thousand volumes all come out of a single mould, and in them there is never any way of keeping clear of lewdness, so that the pages are filled with Pan An, Cao Zijian, Xi Shi and Zhuo Wenjun — all because the author wants to show off the two love poems and flowery rhapsodies he has written himself, and so invents a man and a woman and gives them names, and must needs bring in a petty villain between them to stir up trouble, just like the clown in a play. And the maidservants, no sooner do they open their mouths than it is all 'O' and 'thou' and 'thus' and 'wherefore' — nothing but literary phrases and philosophic talk. So if you look at them one by one, they are all self-contradictory and quite beyond belief, and not to be compared with these few women whom I have seen and heard with my own eyes over half a lifetime. I would not dare to say they surpass all the people in the books of former ages, but the whole story of their lives can dispel sorrow and break through gloom, and there are a few doggerel verses and familiar sayings that can make one laugh aloud over one's wine. As for their partings and meetings, their joys and griefs, their rise and fall and the fortunes they met, I have followed every trace and dared not add the least embellishment, for fear of pleasing men's eyes only to lose the true account. As for the people of today, the poor are worn down by food and clothing day after day, and the rich are troubled by discontent; even if they get a little leisure, they have their lust for pleasure and their craving for goods, their pursuit of trouble — where would they find the time to read books of statecraft? That is why I do not want this story of mine to be praised by the world as marvellous or wonderful, nor do I insist that the world should delight in reading it. I only wish that when men are drunk and sated with pleasure, or when they are escaping the world and putting aside their cares, they might take it up for a while — would that not spare them some of their years and their strength? Better that than scheming after the empty and chasing the false, for it saves one the harm of gossip and quarrels and the weariness of running about on one's feet. Moreover, it may give the world a new vision, unlike those old manuscripts that pull everything in at random, with sudden partings and sudden meetings, pages full of talented scholars and virtuous maidens, of Cao Zijian and Zhuo Wenjun, Hongniang and Xiaoyu, and all the common stock-in-trade. What does my master think of it?"</target>
