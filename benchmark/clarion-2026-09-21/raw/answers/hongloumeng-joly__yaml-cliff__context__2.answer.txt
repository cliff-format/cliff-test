The author says of himself that, having once gone through a dream of illusion, he therefore hides away the true facts, and, borrowing the legend of the “Spirit's Transmigration,” writes this book, The Story of the Stone; hence the name “Zhen Shiyin” [Truth Hidden], and so forth. But what matters, and what persons, does the book record? He himself also says: “Now, toiling dully amid the dust of the world and having accomplished nothing, I suddenly thought of all the women of former days and, examining them one by one with care, felt that their conduct and understanding were all above my own. Why is it that I, a full-grown man with beard and brows, am truly no match for those skirts and hairpins? Truly, shame is more than enough for me, and repentance is of no avail on this day when there is nothing to be done! Wherefore I would fain set down in one volume those past days when I leaned upon the grace of Heaven and the virtue of my ancestors, the days of brocade clothes and silk trousers, of sweet food and rich fare, when I turned my back on the kindness of my father's and brothers' teaching and betrayed the virtue of my teachers' and friends' counsels, down to this day when not one skill is mine and half my life has been spent in failure, and make it known to all men under heaven: my own guilt is indeed not to be escaped; yet within the women's apartments there have always been, plainly to be counted, persons of worth; and by no means must I, because of my own unworthiness, shield my own shortcomings and let them all pass into oblivion. Though today my rafters are of thatch and my windows of mugwort, my stove of brick and my bed of rope, though morning and evening I face wind and dew, and willows by the steps and flowers in the court — these have not yet hindered my heart or my brush. Though I am unlettered and my pen has no art, what harm is there in using feigned words and rustic speech to act out a tale, so that the women's apartments may be made known abroad, and that the world's eyes may be pleased and men's cares dispelled? Is it not fitting?” Hence, too, the name “Jia Yucun” [Feigned Words, Rustic Speech], and so forth."

    target: "The Taoist Kongkong thereupon said to the stone: “Brother Stone, this story of yours — by your own account it has some interest in it, and so you have written it down here, wishing to publish it abroad as a tale for the world. As I see it, in the first place there is no dynasty or year that can be verified; in the second place there is no excellent government of great sages and great loyal ministers to order the court and set right the manners of the age; in it there are nothing but a few out-of-the-way women, some passionate, some foolish, some of small talent and slight virtue, and not one of them with the virtue and ability of Ban Gu's aunt or Cai Wenji. Even if I copy it out and take it away, I fear the world will not care to read it.” The stone laughed and answered: “How foolish my Master is! If you say that no dynasty can be verified, why should you not borrow the reign-years of the Han and the Tang and tack them on? What difficulty would there be in that? But I think that the unofficial histories of every age all tread in one rut, and that it is better for me not to borrow this old frame; on the contrary, I shall be new and strange and distinctive. It is enough to seize the truth of the matter and its natural feeling; why must one be tied down to a dynasty and a year? Moreover, of the common people in the market-place, those who like to read books about ordering government are very few, while those who love agreeable and amusing idle writing are exceedingly many. The unofficial histories of every age either slander rulers and ministers or debase other men's wives and daughters, and their lewdness and violence are past counting. There is, besides, a kind of writing of wind and moon whose filth and stench, whose poisonous brush, ruins men's sons and younger brothers — and these too are past counting. As for books of lovely ladies and gifted scholars, a thousand of them all come out of a single mould, and they can never avoid a taint of lewdness, so that the page is filled with Pan An, Cao Zijian, Xi Shi and Zhuo Wenjun; it is only that the author wants to write out his own two love-poems and flowery rhapsodies, and so he invents the names of a man and a woman, and must needs bring in a petty villain to make trouble between them, just like the clown in a play. And the maids and slave-girls, as soon as they open their mouths, are all “zhe ye zhi hu” — nothing but book-learning and moral principle. So, taken one by one, they are all self-contradictory and quite contrary to reason and feeling, and are not to be compared with these few women whom I have seen with my own eyes and heard with my own ears for half my life. Though I dare not say that they are better than all the persons in the books of former ages, yet the whole course of their affairs can dispel care and break melancholy, and there are also a few indifferent verses and common sayings that can make a man sputter with laughter over his wine. And as for partings and meetings, sorrow and joy, rise and fall and the chances of fortune, these I have followed step by step and traced out, not daring to add a hair of contrivance, and so lose the true tradition for the mere sake of pleasing men's eyes. As for the men of today, the poor are daily burdened by food and clothing, and the rich are discontented at heart; even if they have a moment of leisure, there are still the affairs of lust and lechery, of coveting goods and seeking amusement — where would they find time to read those books about ordering government? Therefore I do not wish the world to call this tale of mine wonderful or marvellous, nor do I insist that the world should delight to read it; I only wish that when they are drunk with wine or lying full-fed, or at the moment when they would shun the world and escape their cares, they might take this and play with it — would it not save them a little of their life's strength and sinew? Compared with scheming after emptiness and chasing after falsehood, it also saves one the harm of gossip and quarrels and of hurrying legs and feet. Moreover, it gives the world new eyes, and is not like those old manuscripts with their random pulling and dragging, their sudden partings and meetings, their pages full of talented men and virtuous ladies, of Zijian and Wenjun, Hongniang and Xiaoyu, and all the rest of the worn-out commonplaces. What does my Master think of it?”"

    target: "“Mean rooms and empty halls — in former years the tablets of office filled the bed; / withered grass and dead willows — here once was a place of song and dance. / Cobwebs hang thick on the carved beams, / and green gauze is now pasted on the matted windows. / What avails it to say that the rouge is rich and the powder fragrant, / when the hair on both temples has turned to frost? / Yesterday the yellow earth of the grave-mound received the white bones; / tonight beneath the red lantern the love-birds lie. / Gold fills the chest and silver fills the chest — / in the twinkling of an eye he is a beggar, and all men revile him. / He is sighing that another's life is short, / little knowing that he himself, when he goes home, will die. / Though he taught his sons with method, he cannot be sure that in after days they will not turn robbers; / though he chose for mates the rich and the well-fed, who would have looked for them to fall into the haunts of flowers and smoke? / Because a gauze cap of office seemed too small, he brought upon himself the cangue and the chain; / yesterday he bemoaned the cold of a tattered coat, today he grumbles that his purple python robe is too long. / In wild confusion, one has scarce done singing when another comes upon the stage, / and the land of others is mistaken for one's own home. / How absurd! In the end, all is but making bridal clothes for other men.” When the mad lame Taoist heard this, he clapped his hands and laughed: “Aptly expounded, aptly expounded!” Shiyin then said but the one word “Come!” and snatching the bundle from the Taoist's shoulder, he slung it on his own back, and without going home at all went off gaily with the mad Taoist. Forthwith the whole neighbourhood was in a stir, and everyone told it abroad as a piece of news. When Madame Feng heard the tidings, she wept till she was more dead than alive, and could only take counsel with her father and send men everywhere to seek for him — but what news was there to be had? There was nothing for it but to depend upon her parents for her daily bread. Fortunately she had still two old maidservants to wait upon her, and mistress and maids, the three of them, spent day and night at needlework, which they sold to help towards her father's expenses. And though Feng Su grumbled every day, there was nothing he could do about it."
