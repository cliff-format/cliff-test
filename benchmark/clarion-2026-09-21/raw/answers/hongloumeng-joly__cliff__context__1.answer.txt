The author himself says: because he had once passed through a dream and an illusion, he hid away the true facts, and, under the pretext of the tale of the 'spirit-endowed' stone, wrote this book, The Story of the Stone. Hence the name Zhen Shiyin ('true facts concealed'). But what matters and what persons are recorded in the book? He says again of himself: 'Now, in these dusty days of toil, having accomplished nothing, I suddenly began to think of all the girls of former days; and as I examined them one by one, I felt that their conduct and understanding were all above my own. How is it that I, a full-grown man, am truly not the equal of those maids? Shame, indeed, I have in plenty, but remorse is of no avail — such is the day when there is nothing to be done! Wherefore I would fain set down the life I have lived — the days when I leaned on Heaven's grace and my ancestors' virtue, the days of brocade and silk, of rich meats and sweet fare — and my guilt in betraying the kindness of my father and elders' teaching and the virtue of my teachers' and friends' reproof, down to this day of no accomplishment in any art and half a lifetime of ruin; and make of it one volume to tell the world: my guilt, indeed, I cannot escape; yet in the inner chambers there have always, verily, been worthy persons, and they must not, because of my own unworthiness and my covering of my own short-comings, be buried in oblivion with me. Though today I live among thatch beams and bramble windows, earthen stove and rope bed, and though morning and evening bring wind and dew, and willows by the steps and flowers in the court, yet none of this hinders my heart and my brush. Though I am unlearned and my pen has no art, what matters it if I take idle words and village speech and fashion out a tale? It may make the inner chambers known to all, and it may gladden the world's eyes and dispel men's sorrow — is that not fitting?' Hence the name Jia Yucun ('idle words and village speech')."

target: "The Taoist of the Great Void then said to the stone: 'Brother Stone, this tale of yours — by your own account it has some interest in it, and so you have written it down here, wishing to publish it as a marvel to the world. In my view, in the first place, there is no dynasty or date to be verified; in the second, there is no great worth or great loyalty, no good government of court or reform of the customs; among it there are nothing but a few strange women — some passionate, some foolish, some of small talent and slight goodness — and not one with the virtue and ability of Ban Zhao or Cai Yan. Even if I copy it out and carry it off, I fear the world will not care to read it.' The stone laughed and answered: 'How foolish my master is! If you say there is no dynasty to be verified, what difficulty is there for my master in borrowing the reign-titles of the Han, the Tang, and the like and adding them on? But I think that the wild histories of every age all tread in one rut; better that mine, which borrows no such conventions, should be new and singular instead. It takes only the substance and reason of the matter; why must one be so bound by dynasty and date? Moreover, among the common people of the market-place, those who love to read books of government are very few, while those who love pleasant and amusing tales are many. The wild histories of every age either slander rulers and ministers or defame men's wives and daughters; lewdness and wickedness are beyond count. There is another sort — the writings of wind and moon — whose filthy obscenity and poisonous brush ruin men's sons and younger brothers, also beyond count. As for the books of beauties and men of genius, a thousand volumes come out of one mould, and in them there is in the end no keeping clear of lewdness, so that the whole page is filled with Pan An, Zi Jian, Xi Shi, and Wen Jun — the author merely wants to show off his own two love-poems and flowery rhapsodies, and so he invents the names of a man and a woman, and must needs bring in a petty fellow between them to stir up confusion, just like the clown in a play. And the maids and serving-girls, as soon as they open their mouths, speak nothing but classical particles and bookish phrases; it is all letters and moral reason. Looked at one by one, they are all self-contradictory and utterly contrary to reason and feeling, and not to be compared with these few women whom I have seen and heard with my own eyes for half a lifetime. Though I dare not say they are better than all the people in the books of former ages, yet the circumstances of their affairs can also dispel sorrow and break melancholy; and there are a few indifferent verses and familiar sayings that can make one laugh and serve for wine. As for meetings and partings, grief and joy, rise and fall and the chances of life, these follow close upon the tracks of truth; I dare not add the least invention, merely to please men's eyes and so lose the true transmission. Men of today: the poor are daily worn out by food and clothing, the rich are again filled with discontent; even if they have a little leisure for a while, there are lust and lechery, love of gain and the seeking of sorrows — where would they find the leisure to read those books of government? So this tale of mine — I do not wish the world to call it marvellous, nor do I insist on the world's delighting to read it; I only wish that when they are drunk and sated with wine, or when they would flee the world and escape their sorrows, they might take it up and play with it — would that not save them some span of life and strength? It is better than scheming after vanities and chasing after delusions, and it saves them the harm of gossip and quarrels and the toil of running about on foot.
